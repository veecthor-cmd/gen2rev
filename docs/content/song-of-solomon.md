## Setting / Era

Song of Solomon (also titled Song of Songs, or Canticles) is a poetic book, not a narrative one —
a collection of love poems exchanged between two voices, traditionally associated with Solomon and
a beloved ("the Shulammite," 6:13), though the text itself narrates no plot events the way Genesis
or Joshua do. Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md`'s Wisdom/Poetry grouping and Leviticus's precedent
(`docs/reviews/leviticus-review.md`), this brief is built around **pivotal passages**, not scenes.

**This book's fundamental interpretive lens is itself a live, centuries-old, cross-tradition
question this brief does not resolve and no challenge item may assume an answer to.** Jewish
tradition — from the Talmudic era onward, following Rabbi Akiva's declaration that "the Song of
Songs is the Holy of Holies" — has long read the book allegorically, as the love between God and
Israel; a large stream of historic Christian tradition (Origen, Bernard of Clairvaux, and
centuries of commentary since) reads it typologically or allegorically as Christ's love for the
Church. Modern historical-critical scholarship, and many contemporary readers across traditions,
read the book as a literal celebration of human romantic and marital love, drawing on parallels
with other ancient Near Eastern love poetry; some contemporary scholarship proposes a figural
reading that holds both together rather than choosing one. **After roughly twenty centuries there
is still no consensus, and this brief takes no position.** Every passage selected below reads
coherently and is presented in a way compatible with either the allegorical or the literal
reading — the text itself, quoted verbatim, not this brief's (or the game's) resolution of what it
"ultimately" means.

**Given the sensitivity flagged in `CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §6 (Wisdom/Poetry row) and the product's
guest-play, no-signup, informally-shared distribution model (`PRODUCT_REQUIREMENTS.md` §2), this
brief deliberately leans toward the book's most universally-safe material** — its refrains about
love's timing, its abstract nature/garden imagery, and its closing declaration of love's power —
and **explicitly excludes** the book's physical-description poems entirely (see Contested
Territory below). This is a considered scope decision, following this project's precedent with
2 Samuel's Bathsheba scene and 1 Kings 3:16 (narrative-summary-only, sensitive detail never
displayed as challenge text).

## Pivotal Passages (4)

### 1. The Refrain — "Do Not Awaken Love Until It Desires" (Song of Solomon 2:7, 3:5, 8:4)
A three-times-repeated adjuration, spoken to "the daughters of Jerusalem," not to force or rush
love before its own time. The single most quoted, least contested line in the book across
traditions.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Song of Solomon 2:7 (WEB): "I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, or by the hinds
  of the field, that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires."
- Song of Solomon 3:5 (WEB): "I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, by the roes, or by the hinds
  of the field, that you not stir up nor awaken love, until it so desires."
- Song of Solomon 8:4 (WEB): "I adjure you, daughters of Jerusalem, that you not stir up, nor
  awaken love, until it so desires."

*(Note the WEB wording is not identical across the three occurrences — 8:4 drops the "by the
roes... field" clause present in 2:7 and 3:5. This is the text as attested, not an error; verbatim
QA should confirm all three independently rather than assuming they're one repeated string.)*

### 2. The Voice of Spring (Song of Solomon 2:10-13)
An invitation into a springtime scene — winter past, flowers appearing, the turtledove's voice
heard, the fig tree ripening. Nature imagery held at the level of season and landscape, not the
body-as-garden imagery found elsewhere in the book (see Contested Territory).

**Challenge-ready verse:**
- Song of Solomon 2:10-13 (WEB): "My beloved spoke, and said to me, 'Rise up, my love, my
  beautiful one, and come away. For behold, the winter is past. The rain is over and gone. The
  flowers appear on the earth. The time of the singing has come, and the voice of the turtledove
  is heard in our land. The fig tree ripens her green figs. The vines are in blossom. They give
  out their fragrance. Arise, my love, my beautiful one, and come away.'"

### 3. Mutual Belonging (Song of Solomon 2:16, trimmed)
A short declaration of mutual, exclusive devotion — one of the book's most-quoted lines outside
its original context (weddings, needlework samplers, devotional literature).

**Challenge-ready verse:**
- Song of Solomon 2:16a (WEB, trimmed): "My beloved is mine, and I am his."
  *(The verse's second sentence, "He browses among the lilies," is deliberately excluded — see
  Contested Territory. Never display or grade the full verse; the trim point is the sentence
  boundary, not a mid-sentence cut.)*

### 4. Love Strong as Death (Song of Solomon 8:6-7)
The book's climactic declaration about love's power, widely quoted across traditions with minimal
controversy over the text's plain sense (see Contested Territory for the one narrow textual note
that does apply).

**Challenge-ready verse:**
- Song of Solomon 8:6-7 (WEB): "Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm; for love is
  strong as death. Jealousy is as cruel as Sheol. Its flashes are flashes of fire, a very flame of
  Yah. Many waters can't quench love, neither can floods drown it. If a man would give all the
  wealth of his house for love, he would be utterly scorned."

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here.
This section is longer than usual for this project given the assignment's explicit instruction
that this book "gets its own fully dedicated pass... not a rubber stamp."

- **Whole-book interpretive framing (allegorical vs. literal).** See Setting/Era above. This is
  not a single-verse issue like Genesis 3:15 or Genesis 22 — it's a live disagreement about what
  kind of book this fundamentally is, spanning Jewish tradition (allegory of God/Israel), historic
  Christian tradition (allegory/typology of Christ/Church), and modern scholarship (literal human
  love poetry, or a figural reading holding both together). No challenge item, flavor text, or
  world description may assert either reading as the book's "real" meaning. **This is the primary
  reason this brief is being escalated rather than self-approved** — see the review file.
- **Deliberately excluded: the body-description poems (Song of Solomon 4, 6, 7) and the opening
  "kisses of his mouth" (1:2).** Per the assignment's explicit instruction, no challenge item is
  built from these chapters. This mirrors the project's existing precedent with 2 Samuel's
  Bathsheba scene and 1 Kings 3:16 — narrative-summary-only, sensitive detail never displayed as
  challenge text. Unlike those two cases, here the exclusion isn't a summary standing in for a
  scene — the passages above simply don't touch this material at all. No verse from these chapters
  appears anywhere in this brief.
- **The refrain's immediate context (2:6, 3:4, 8:3).** Each occurrence of the "do not awaken love"
  refrain (Passage 1) is immediately preceded in the text by an embrace description ("His left
  hand is under my head. His right hand embraces me," 2:6/8:3). **The challenge-ready text starts
  at 2:7/3:5/8:4, not the preceding verse** — this boundary is deliberate and must not be loosened
  by a future authoring pass that assumes "a little more context" is harmless.
- **Song of Solomon 2:16's second clause, "He browses among the lilies."** Confirmed via multiple
  mainstream Christian commentaries (e.g. [Enduring Word on Song of Solomon 2](https://enduringword.com/bible-commentary/song-of-solomon-2/),
  [Together in Christ](https://www.heartlight.org/togetherinchrist/0407.html)) as read by a
  significant body of commentary as a euphemism for physical intimacy, paralleling the same phrase
  in 6:2-3. The brief trims Passage 3 to the verse's first sentence only for this reason.
- **Song of Solomon 8:6's "a very flame of Yah."** A live scholarly (not denominational) dispute:
  some scholars read the Hebrew *shalhebetyah* as containing a truncated form of the divine name
  YHWH — which, if correct, would make this the only reference to God anywhere in the book — while
  others read the "-yah" ending as an intensifying superlative with no divine-name content at all.
  [TheTorah.com, "YHWH's Flame: A Love Metaphor in the Song of Songs"](https://www.thetorah.com/article/yhwhs-flame-a-love-metaphor-in-the-song-of-songs)
  lays out both positions. The challenge item built from this verse (Passage 4) tests recall of the
  verbatim WEB text only — "a very flame of Yah" as written — and never asks what "Yah" refers to
  or whether this verse names God. No change needed, but flagged so a future authoring pass doesn't
  build an interpretive item on top of this ambiguity.
- **General-audience / child-safety distribution risk (whole book, not one passage).** Per
  `PRODUCT_REQUIREMENTS.md` §2, links are shared informally with no signup or age gate, including
  plausibly into family group chats. Even the material selected above — none of which is graphic —
  is thematically about romantic desire and physical affection in a way no other book in this
  project's 21 already-shipped worlds is. This brief does not resolve whether that thematic
  register, however carefully bounded, is appropriate for the product's general/"Disciple"-tier,
  no-forced-signup distribution model; that judgment call is what's being escalated.
