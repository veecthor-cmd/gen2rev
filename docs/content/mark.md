## Setting / Era

Mark is the shortest and, per the modern scholarly majority (the "Markan priority" position), the
earliest of the four Gospels — likely written for a Gentile, probably Roman, audience around AD
65-70, during or immediately after the turbulence of the Jewish revolt against Rome (this dating
rests on how Mark 13's discourse about Jerusalem's coming destruction is read; this brief takes no
position on the dating debate itself, which is a historical-critical question, not a doctrinal
one). Church tradition going back to Papias (early second century, preserved by Eusebius) holds
that Mark, an associate of Peter, wrote down Peter's preaching; modern critical scholarship
generally treats this tradition as unverifiable rather than confirmed, while not being able to
disprove it either — this brief does not take a position on authorship, since nothing in this
project's content depends on it. What is not in question, and what actually shapes this brief, is
the finished text's own distinctive character, especially set against Matthew, Luke, and John
(worlds 40, 42, 43), which this project is building in the same wave:

- **No birth narrative.** Unlike Matthew and Luke, Mark opens with no genealogy, no
  annunciation, no manger — it begins in medias res with John the Baptist in the wilderness (1:1-8)
  and Jesus's baptism (1:9-11). World 41 opens where Mark opens.
- **Breathless pace.** Mark's Greek uses the word *euthys* ("immediately"/"straightway") far more
  often, proportional to its length, than any other Gospel — action follows action with almost no
  pause for discourse, especially in chapters 1-8. Several challenge-ready verses below preserve
  this word in the WEB text so the pacing is visible in the game itself, not just described in
  prose.
- **The "messianic secret."** Jesus repeatedly silences demons, healed people, and even his own
  disciples about who he is (1:34, 1:44, 3:12, 5:43, 7:36, 8:30, 9:9) — a recurring narrative
  pattern distinctive to how Mark tells the story (Matthew and Luke keep some of these moments but
  do not build the same sustained pattern around them). This brief treats it as exactly that: an
  observable, repeated feature of what the text narrates Jesus doing and saying — not a claim about
  why Mark included it, which is a historical-critical question this brief does not adjudicate.
- **Unsparing about the disciples' failure to understand.** Mark is generally read as more blunt
  than Matthew or Luke about the Twelve's incomprehension and fear (4:40-41, 6:52, 8:17-21,
  9:32, 14:50) — this brief draws on that material directly rather than smoothing it over.
- **The ending.** The earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts end at 16:8, with the women
  fleeing the empty tomb in fear and saying nothing to anyone — genuinely abrupt, and genuinely
  disputed as to whether that was Mark's intended ending or the book was damaged/left unfinished.
  Verses 16:9-20 are a widely-attested but almost certainly later addition. This is a real,
  documented textual-criticism question (not this brief's invention or opinion) — handled directly
  in Scene 13 and in Contested Territory below, not smoothed over or skipped.

No historicity, dating, or authorship claim is asserted or required by this brief; see Contested
Territory below for the handling of every genuinely disputed item.

## Pivotal Scenes (13)

### 1. No Birth Narrative — John the Baptist and Jesus's Baptism (Mark 1:1-13)
Mark opens not with Jesus's birth but with "the beginning of the Good News," identifying John the
Baptist as the fulfillment of a wilderness-preparation prophecy, then narrates Jesus's baptism: the
heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice from heaven declares him God's beloved
Son. Immediately afterward (again, Mark's word), the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness, where
he is tempted by Satan for forty days.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 1:1 (WEB): "The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God."
- Mark 1:9-11 (WEB): "In those days, Jesus came from Nazareth of Galilee, and was baptized by John
  in the Jordan. Immediately coming up from the water, he saw the heavens parting and the Spirit
  descending on him like a dove. A voice came out of the sky, 'You are my beloved Son, in whom I am
  well pleased.'"
- Mark 1:12-13 (WEB): "Immediately the Spirit drove him out into the wilderness. He was there in
  the wilderness forty days, tempted by Satan. He was with the wild animals; and the angels were
  serving him."

### 2. A Day in Capernaum: Calling the First Disciples, and the Messianic Secret Begins (Mark 1:16-45)
In a single fast-moving sequence, Jesus calls Simon, Andrew, James, and John away from their nets,
teaches in the Capernaum synagogue "as one having authority," casts out an unclean spirit that
correctly names him and is silenced, heals Simon's mother-in-law, heals and casts out demons from a
crowd at sundown while refusing to let the demons speak "because they knew him," and rises before
dawn to pray alone. The chapter closes with a healed leper who was told to say nothing but
proclaimed it anyway, so that Jesus "could no more openly enter into a city."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 1:17 (WEB): "Jesus said to them, 'Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men.'"
- Mark 1:22 (WEB): "They were astonished at his teaching, for he taught them as having authority,
  and not as the scribes."
- Mark 1:34 (WEB): "He healed many who were sick with various diseases and cast out many demons. He
  didn't allow the demons to speak, because they knew him."
- Mark 1:35 (WEB): "Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and
  departed into a deserted place, and prayed there."

### 3. Authority to Forgive Sins: The Paralytic Lowered Through the Roof (Mark 2:1-12)
Four men, unable to reach Jesus through the crowd, dig through a roof to lower their paralyzed
friend down to him. Jesus first declares the man's sins forgiven — provoking religious leaders'
private objection that only God can forgive sins — then heals him physically as proof of "the Son
of Man['s] authority on earth to forgive sins," and the man walks out carrying his own mat.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 2:5 (WEB): "Jesus, seeing their faith, said to the paralytic, 'Son, your sins are forgiven
  you.'"
- Mark 2:9-10 (WEB, trimmed): "Which is easier, to tell the paralytic, 'Your sins are forgiven;' or
  to say, 'Arise, and take up your bed, and walk'? But that you may know that the Son of Man has
  authority on earth to forgive sins" *(Verse 10 continues "—he said to the paralytic—" before the
  actual healing command, which falls in v11 and is not part of this citation; trimmed here at the
  clause boundary, per the independent verbatim-text QA pass — `docs/qa/mark-verbatim-qa.md`.)*
- Mark 2:12 (WEB): "He arose, and immediately took up the mat and went out in front of them all, so
  that they were all amazed and glorified God, saying, 'We never saw anything like this!'"

### 4. The Purpose of Parables, and Calming the Storm (Mark 4:1-41)
Jesus teaches in parables from a boat, including the parable of the sower, and privately tells his
disciples that the parables' purpose is bound up with who does and doesn't perceive their meaning.
That evening, crossing the lake, a violent storm terrifies the disciples; Jesus rebukes the wind and
sea with a word, and the disciples respond not with relief but with a deeper fear: "Who then is
this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?"

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 4:11-12 (WEB): "To you is given the mystery of God's Kingdom, but to those who are outside,
  all things are done in parables, that 'seeing they may see and not perceive, and hearing they may
  hear and not understand, lest perhaps they should turn again, and their sins should be forgiven
  them.'" *(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory: Mark 4:11-12.)*
- Mark 4:39 (WEB): "He awoke and rebuked the wind, and said to the sea, 'Peace! Be still!' The wind
  ceased and there was a great calm."
- Mark 4:41 (WEB): "They were greatly afraid and said to one another, 'Who then is this, that even
  the wind and the sea obey him?'"

### 5. A Markan Sandwich: Jairus's Daughter and the Woman with the Flow of Blood (Mark 5:21-43)
A synagogue leader, Jairus, begs Jesus to heal his dying daughter. On the way, a woman who has
suffered twelve years of bleeding touches Jesus's clothing in the crowd and is healed; Jesus stops
to identify her and commends her faith. The delay means Jairus's daughter dies before Jesus arrives
— but Jesus raises her anyway, with a simple Aramaic command the text preserves and translates.
This "story within a story" structure (one narrative interrupted by a second, related one, then
resumed) recurs elsewhere in Mark (e.g. the fig tree and the temple in ch. 11, Scene 10) and is
widely noted as a distinctive feature of how Mark builds his narrative.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 5:23 (WEB): "My little daughter is at the point of death. Please come and lay your hands on
  her, that she may be made healthy, and live."
- Mark 5:27-28 (WEB): "having heard the things concerning Jesus, came up behind him in the crowd
  and touched his clothes. For she said, 'If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well.'"
- Mark 5:34 (WEB): "He said to her, 'Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be
  cured of your disease.'"
- Mark 5:41-42 (WEB, trimmed): "Taking the child by the hand, he said to her, 'Talitha cumi!' which
  means, being interpreted, 'Girl, I tell you, get up!' Immediately the girl rose up and walked, for
  she was twelve years old." *(Verse 42 continues "They were amazed with great amazement," a general
  reaction phrase not needed for this citation's narrative-fact point; trimmed here, per the
  independent verbatim-text QA pass — `docs/qa/mark-verbatim-qa.md`.)*

### 6. Feeding the Five Thousand, Walking on Water, and Hardened Hearts (Mark 6:30-52)
Jesus feeds a crowd of five thousand from five loaves and two fish; that night he walks on the sea
to reach his disciples in a storm, telling them "It is I. Don't be afraid." Mark adds a line
without a parallel this pointed elsewhere: the disciples were "astounded" not because of who Jesus
plainly was, but because they "hadn't understood about the loaves" — their hearts, the text says
plainly, "were hardened."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 6:41-42 (WEB): "He took the five loaves and the two fish; and looking up to heaven, he
  blessed and broke the loaves, and he gave to his disciples to set before them, and he divided the
  two fish among them all. They all ate and were filled."
- Mark 6:50 (WEB): "But he immediately spoke with them and said to them, 'Cheer up! It is I! Don't
  be afraid.'"
- Mark 6:52 (WEB): "for they hadn't understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened."

### 7. The Two-Stage Healing of the Blind Man at Bethsaida (Mark 8:22-26)
A blind man is brought to Jesus at Bethsaida. Jesus touches his eyes once — the man sees, but only
"men... like walking trees" — then touches him a second time, and he sees clearly. This healing has
no parallel in Matthew or Luke and is widely read (though this brief does not grade that reading —
see Contested Territory) as Mark placing a literal picture of gradual, two-stage seeing right before
a section (8:27-10:52) about the disciples' own gradual, incomplete grasp of who Jesus is, bracketed
at the far end by a second, sudden healing of a blind man (Bartimaeus, Scene 10) who "immediately"
sees and follows.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 8:23-25 (WEB): "When he had spat on his eyes, and laid his hands on him, he asked him if he
  saw anything. He looked up, and said, 'I see men, but I see them like walking trees.' Then again
  he laid his hands on his eyes. He looked intently, and was restored, and saw everyone clearly."

### 8. Peter's Confession, the First Passion Prediction, and the Transfiguration (Mark 8:27-9:9)
At Caesarea Philippi, Jesus asks who people say he is, then who the disciples say he is; Peter
answers, "You are the Christ." Jesus immediately orders them to tell no one, then states plainly for
the first time that the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and rise after three days.
When Peter objects, Jesus rebukes him sharply. Six days later, Jesus is transfigured before Peter,
James, and John; a cloud and a voice again declare him God's Son, and as they come down the
mountain, Jesus again orders silence — this time with an end point attached: "until after the Son
of Man had risen from the dead."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 8:29 (WEB): "Peter answered, 'You are the Christ.'"
- Mark 8:30 (WEB): "He commanded them that they should tell no one about him."
- Mark 8:31 (WEB): "He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be
  rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days
  rise again."
- Mark 8:33 (WEB): "But he, turning around and seeing his disciples, rebuked Peter, and said, 'Get
  behind me, Satan! For you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men.'"
- Mark 9:7 (WEB): "A cloud came, overshadowing them, and a voice came out of the cloud, 'This is my
  beloved Son. Listen to him.'"
- Mark 9:9 (WEB): "As they were coming down from the mountain, he commanded them that they should
  tell no one what things they had seen, until after the Son of Man had risen from the dead."

### 9. The Way to Jerusalem: Divorce, the Rich Young Man, and the Ransom Saying (Mark 10:1-45)
Heading toward Jerusalem, Jesus teaches on marriage and divorce, grounding it in Genesis's creation
account ("what God has joined together, let no man separate"). A man asks what he must do to
inherit eternal life; Jesus, having listed several commandments, tells him — because the text says
Jesus "loved him" — to sell what he has and follow him, and the man goes away sad, for he was rich.
Jesus tells his disciples how hard it is for the wealthy to enter God's Kingdom, and when they ask
who then can be saved, he answers: "With men it is impossible, but not with God, for all things are
possible with God." On the road, Jesus predicts his death and resurrection a third time in stark
detail, and states his mission in one of Mark's most quoted single verses: the Son of Man came "to
give his life as a ransom for many."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 10:6-9 (WEB): "God made them male and female. For this cause a man will leave his father and
  mother, and will join to his wife, and the two will become one flesh, so that they are no longer
  two, but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." *(Handled per
  the format restriction below — see Contested Territory: Mark 10:1-12.)*
- Mark 10:17-18 (WEB): "'Good Teacher, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?' Jesus said
  to him, 'Why do you call me good? No one is good except one—God.'"
- Mark 10:21 (WEB): "Jesus looking at him loved him, and said to him, 'One thing you lack. Go, sell
  whatever you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow
  me, taking up the cross.'" *(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory:
  Mark 10:17-31.)*
- Mark 10:27 (WEB): "Jesus, looking at them, said, 'With men it is impossible, but not with God, for
  all things are possible with God.'"
- Mark 10:33-34 (WEB): "Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem. The Son of Man will be delivered to
  the chief priests and the scribes. They will condemn him to death, and will deliver him to the
  Gentiles. They will mock him, spit on him, scourge him, and kill him. On the third day he will
  rise again."
- Mark 10:45 (WEB): "For the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his
  life as a ransom for many." *(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory:
  Mark 10:45.)*

### 10. Blind Bartimaeus and the Triumphal Entry (Mark 10:46-11:11)
Leaving Jericho, a blind beggar, Bartimaeus, cries out to "Jesus, son of David" for mercy despite
the crowd's rebuke; Jesus calls him over, and — unlike the two-touch healing at Bethsaida — heals
him with a word. Bartimaeus immediately regains his sight and "followed him on the way," a phrase
this project treats as pure narrative fact (what the text says he did), completing the sight/
discipleship bracket begun in Scene 7. Jesus then enters Jerusalem on a donkey to crowds shouting
"Hosanna" and blessing "the coming kingdom of our father David."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 10:47-48 (WEB): "When he heard that it was Jesus the Nazarene, he began to cry out and say,
  'Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!' Many rebuked him, that he should be quiet, but he
  cried out much more, 'You son of David, have mercy on me!'"
- Mark 10:52 (WEB): "Jesus said to him, 'Go your way. Your faith has made you well.' Immediately he
  received his sight and followed Jesus on the way."
- Mark 11:9-10 (WEB): "Those who went in front and those who followed cried out, 'Hosanna! Blessed
  is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the kingdom of our father David that is
  coming in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!'"

### 11. The Last Supper and Gethsemane (Mark 14:12-50)
At a Passover meal, Jesus takes bread and a cup, identifying them with his body and "blood of the
covenant, poured out for many." Afterward in Gethsemane, deeply distressed, he asks that "this cup"
be removed if possible, but submits to what God wills. Betrayed and arrested, "they all left him,
and fled" — the text's own blunt statement of the disciples' collapse.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 14:22-24 (WEB): "As they were eating, Jesus took bread, and when he had blessed it, he broke
  it and gave to them, and said, 'Take, eat. This is my body.' He took the cup, and when he had
  given thanks, he gave to them. They all drank of it. He said to them, 'This is my blood of the
  new covenant, which is poured out for many.'" *(Handled per the format restriction below — see
  Contested Territory: Mark 14:22-24.)*
- Mark 14:36 (WEB): "He said, 'Abba, Father, all things are possible to you. Please remove this cup
  from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire.'"
- Mark 14:50 (WEB): "They all left him, and fled."

### 12. Trial, Crucifixion, and the Temple Curtain Torn (Mark 14:53-15:39)
Before the Sanhedrin, the high priest asks Jesus directly whether he is "the Christ, the Son of the
Blessed"; Jesus answers, "I am" — a rare moment where the silence of the "messianic secret" is
broken, on the way to his condemnation. Handed to Pilate and crucified, Jesus cries out in Aramaic
words the text translates as "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" At his death, the temple
curtain tears from top to bottom, and a Roman centurion — an outsider, not a disciple — declares,
"Truly this man was the Son of God!"

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 14:61-62 (WEB): "'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?' Jesus said, 'I am. You will
  see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky.'"
- Mark 15:34 (WEB): "At the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, 'Eloi, Eloi, lama
  sabachthani?' which is, being interpreted, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'"
- Mark 15:38 (WEB): "The veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom."
- Mark 15:39 (WEB): "When the centurion, who stood by opposite him, saw that he cried out like this
  and breathed his last, he said, 'Truly this man was the Son of God!'"

### 13. The Empty Tomb and Mark's Abrupt Ending (Mark 16:1-8)
Three women come to anoint Jesus's body and find the tomb open and empty. A young man in white
tells them Jesus has risen and instructs them to tell the disciples and Peter that he goes ahead of
them to Galilee. In the earliest and most reliable manuscripts, the book ends there, on an
unresolved, unsettling note: "They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid." See Contested
Territory below for the full handling of what follows (or doesn't) after verse 8 — this brief does
not select any verse from 16:9-20 as challenge-ready content, for reasons stated there.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Mark 16:6 (WEB): "He said to them, 'Don't be amazed. You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been
  crucified. He has risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid him!'"
- Mark 16:8 (WEB): "They went out, and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had come
  on them. They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid."

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 and §6 (New Testament Addendum) — flagged for the theological
reviewer, not resolved here. Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7, the cross-tradition check below
weights Protestant/Catholic/Orthodox sources as the primary axis, not the OT's Jewish-tradition
axis.

- **Mark 16:9-20, the "Longer Ending" — a live manuscript-tradition (textual-criticism) issue,
  handled directly, not skipped.** This is one of the three named textual-criticism cases in the
  style guide addendum. Checked live: **the WEB text at [ebible.org](https://ebible.org/web/MRK16.htm)
  and [BibleGateway](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark+16&version=WEB) both print
  verses 9-20 in full**, but both carry a translators' footnote at verse 9 stating that "NU includes
  the text of verses 9-20, but mentions in a footnote that a few manuscripts omitted it," and that
  WEB's translators "regard Mark 16:9-20 as reliable based on an overwhelming majority of textual
  evidence, including... the authoritative Greek Majority Text New Testament... the TR and many of
  the manuscripts cited in the NU text" — i.e., WEB's inclusion reflects a deliberate
  Majority-Text-priority translation philosophy, not an absence of the underlying textual dispute.
  That dispute is real and long-documented independent of WEB's editorial choice: **this brief does
  not select any verse from 16:9-20 as challenge-ready content.** Full cross-tradition sourcing and
  reasoning in `docs/reviews/mark-review.md`.
- **Mark 10:1-12, teaching on divorce.** Genuine, live, three-way tradition split confirmed via
  live sourcing (full detail in the review file): Catholic teaching (Catechism §1614-1615) reads
  this as establishing marriage's strict indissolubility, permitting no divorce with remarriage
  (only annulment, a declaration a valid marriage never existed); mainstream Protestant/evangelical
  teaching generally reads Matthew's parallel "exception clause" (not present in Mark's own text) as
  permitting divorce and remarriage in cases of sexual immorality or, per 1 Corinthians 7:15,
  abandonment by an unbelieving spouse; Orthodox teaching permits a second or even third marriage
  through "economia" (pastoral concession), with a penitential remarriage rite. **This brief's
  bounding:** Mark 10:6-9 is used only in recall/fill-in-blank format — testing the verbatim text
  itself — never a multiple-choice item about what circumstances permit divorce or remarriage, which
  Mark's own text in this passage does not itself address (that question arises only from reading
  Matthew's parallel and later tradition alongside it).
- **Mark 10:17-31, the rich young man and eternal life.** Touches the faith-vs-works soteriological
  fault line the style guide addendum names directly. Catholic tradition (per the Catholic
  Encyclopedia's "Evangelical Counsels" article, citing this very passage) reads Jesus's instruction
  as the scriptural root of the "counsels of perfection" (poverty, chastity, obedience) — a path to
  a fuller following of Christ, explicitly not framed as necessary for salvation itself, but
  historically the basis for the church's monastic/religious life tradition. Mainstream Protestant
  commentary (surveyed via BibleHub's cross-tradition aggregation) reads the instruction as a
  particular, situational test aimed at exposing this one man's attachment to wealth, explicitly not
  a general work required for salvation — with salvation's real basis stated in the passage's own
  next lines ("with men it is impossible, but not with God"). **This brief's bounding:** the
  challenge items here test only the narrative facts (what Jesus said, that the man went away sad,
  what Jesus said afterward about wealth and God's power to save) — never a multiple-choice item
  asking what is required for salvation or how this passage relates to faith versus works.
- **Mark 10:45, "a ransom for many" — atonement theory.** This is the single clearest atonement-
  theory flashpoint in Mark's own text, confirmed via live sourcing: this exact verse is the primary
  proof-text cited, differently, by ransom theory (the price is paid to Satan, an early patristic and
  Orthodox-adjacent reading), Christus Victor (Christ's death as victory over the powers of evil,
  without a "payment to Satan" framing), and penal substitution (mainstream evangelical/Reformed
  reading, e.g. The Gospel Coalition, connecting the verse to Isaiah 53's suffering-servant language)
  — three different accounts of how and to whom the "ransom" is paid, all claiming this same verse.
  **This brief's bounding:** the challenge item tests only the verbatim text (what Jesus said his
  purpose was, in his own stated words: to serve and to give his life as a ransom for many) — never
  a multiple-choice item about the mechanism or recipient of the ransom.
- **Mark 14:22-24, the Last Supper — sacramental theology.** Genuine, live, multi-way tradition
  split confirmed via sourcing: Catholic teaching holds transubstantiation (the bread and wine
  become Christ's body and blood in substance); Orthodox teaching affirms a true, real, substantial
  presence while explicitly declining to define the change's mechanism (per St. Philaret's
  catechism); Lutheran teaching holds a real presence "in, with, and under" the elements
  ("sacramental union," sometimes loosely called consubstantiation); Reformed/Calvinist teaching
  holds a spiritual, faith-appropriated presence; Zwinglian/Baptist/much of broader evangelical
  tradition holds a memorial view with no special presence. **This brief's bounding:** the challenge
  item is the verbatim institution narrative only — what Jesus said and did — never a multiple-choice
  item about what happens to the bread and wine or what the meal accomplishes.
- **Mark 4:11-12, the stated purpose of parables.** This verse states, in Jesus's own words, that
  parables are given so that some "may see and not perceive... lest perhaps they should turn again,
  and their sins should be forgiven them" — language that intersects predestination/free-will
  territory (this project already excluded Malachi 1:2-3 on the same grounds, per
  `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's Romans 9 note) if pressed into a claim about why some people do or
  don't believe. **This brief's bounding:** used in recall/fill-in-blank format only, testing the
  verbatim text, never a multiple-choice "what does this mean" or "why are some excluded" format.
- **The "messianic secret" motif and direct divine-sonship declarations (1:11, 3:11, 8:29-30, 9:7,
  14:61-62, 15:39).** Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's framing: unlike the OT, where nearly every
  exclusion in this project worked by excluding a *later* Christian reading layered onto the text,
  here the claim that Jesus is God's Son **is the primary text itself** — the voice at the baptism
  and transfiguration, the demons' testimony, Peter's confession, the high priest's question and
  Jesus's answer, and the centurion's declaration are all things the text directly narrates as said,
  not later interpretations of something more neutral. This brief treats all of these as in-bounds
  narrative fact — reporting what the text says was said and by whom — exactly as any other narrated
  speech in this project's other books is handled, and does not ask any player to affirm the truth
  of these claims beyond what the text itself states was said. No further doctrinal content (e.g. the
  mechanism or metaphysics of the incarnation, Trinitarian formulation) is added or implied.
- **Ecclesiology/gender-role passages — checked, not applicable.** The style guide addendum names
  this as a default-contested NT category (citing 1 Timothy 2-3, Titus 1, 1 Corinthians 11/14).
  Checked directly: Mark's narrative contains no church-office or gender-role instructional material
  of that kind — that category belongs to the epistles this project has not yet reached. Nothing in
  this brief required bounding on this ground.
- **Spiritual gifts.** The style guide addendum's other named default-contested category (tongues,
  prophecy, healing; cessationism vs. continuationism) is present in Mark only inside the excluded
  16:17-20 material (speaking in new languages, handling serpents, drinking poison unharmed, laying
  hands on the sick) — already excluded above on textual-criticism grounds, which also resolves this
  concern for this brief; no in-bounds verse in this brief requires separate handling here.
- **Mark 13, the Olivet Discourse (not selected for challenge content).** Mark's extended
  eschatological discourse — the coming destruction of the temple, "the abomination of desolation,"
  wars and rumors of wars, the timing question ("this generation will not pass away"), and the
  explicit statement that "no one knows... not even the angels... nor the Son, but the Father"
  (13:32) — is exactly the kind of pre-/mid-/post-tribulation, "this generation," and
  Olivet-timeline territory the style guide addendum names as the single most fragmented
  interpretive ground in Christian theology. This brief deliberately does not select any verse from
  Mark 13 for challenge content, the same scope choice this project's OT briefs have made for
  similarly dense contested material (e.g. Hosea's exclusion of 4-5's fuller indictment material).
  This is a real scene in Mark's own structure and is mentioned narratively in the Setting/Era
  section above (re: dating) but is not built into a numbered pivotal scene or given any
  challenge-ready verse.
- **Mark's ending and 16:9-20's relationship to Matthew, Luke, and John (worlds 40, 42, 43).** Per
  this assignment's differentiation instruction: this brief's emphasis (no birth narrative, the
  breathless pace, the messianic secret, the abrupt 16:8 ending) is drawn from what is genuinely
  distinctive to Mark's own telling, not the material shared with the other three Gospels currently
  being authored in the same wave. Scenes 1, 2, 6's "hardened hearts" note, 7, and 13 in particular
  have no close equivalent in how the other three Gospels tell the same underlying story.
