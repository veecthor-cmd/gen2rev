## Setting / Era

Matthew is the first book of the New Testament and opens the Gospels section of the canon
(`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7, Wave 1 — worlds 40-43, one Gospel per authoring agent). It narrates
the life, teaching, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, written — per near-universal
scholarly consensus on the book's internal features (its opening genealogy rooting Jesus in
Abraham and David, its ~10 "fulfillment" formula quotations tying events to Hebrew Scripture, its
sustained engagement with Pharisaic debate, and its untranslated Jewish terms) — for a
Jewish-Christian audience concerned with showing that Jesus is Israel's promised Messiah,
continuous with and fulfilling the Law and the Prophets rather than replacing them.

**What makes this brief distinctively Matthew's Gospel, not a generic Gospel harmony:** three
things structurally unique to Matthew drive this brief's emphasis, per this assignment's
instruction that Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John are being authored in parallel and will play
back-to-back (worlds 40-43) — a player should not experience the same four beats four times.

1. **The fulfillment-quotation formula.** Matthew alone repeatedly interrupts the narrative with
   "this was to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet..." — a structural device found nowhere
   else in the Gospels at this density. Seven such formula quotations are included below (1:22-23,
   2:15, 2:17-18, 2:23, 4:14-16, 8:17, 21:4-5), each handled per the Contested Territory section's
   Fulfillment-Quotation Formula rule.
2. **The Sermon on the Mount (chs. 5-7).** Matthew's extended teaching block — the Beatitudes, the
   Lord's Prayer, the Golden Rule — appears nowhere else in the New Testament at this length (Luke's
   parallel "Sermon on the Plain," Luke 6, is much shorter and differently structured). This brief
   gives it four scenes (6-9 below), proportionate to its length and centrality to Matthew
   specifically.
3. **Church-order material unique to Matthew.** The "keys of the kingdom" (16:19) and the
   step-by-step church-discipline procedure (18:15-20) — including the only two uses of the word
   *ekklēsia* ("assembly"/"church") anywhere in the Gospels — appear only in Matthew among the four
   Gospels. Both are included below, bounded per Contested Territory.

The brief still gives Matthew's own complete narrative arc — birth, baptism, teaching, miracles,
opposition, death, and resurrection — so a player who only ever plays World 40 gets a coherent
story; it simply weights space toward what is *Matthew's own telling* of shared events (the
fulfillment framing, the Jewish-audience argument) rather than treating the miracles/passion/
resurrection beats shared with Mark/Luke/John as this brief's centerpiece.

**Central sensitivity, stated plainly:** per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7, the escape hatch this
project used repeatedly on the OT side — excluding a *later* Christian reading layered onto an OT
text — mostly does not exist here, because claims like the virgin birth and the resurrection are
Matthew's own primary text, not a later reading of it. This brief narrates what Matthew's text
itself states happened, the same way any other narrated event in this project is handled, without
any challenge item asking a player to affirm the claim's truth beyond what the text states.

## Pivotal Scenes (18)

### 1. The Genealogy and the Angel's Announcement to Joseph (Matthew 1:1-25)
Matthew opens not with Jesus's birth but with a genealogy rooting him in Abraham and David,
explicitly structured (1:17) as three sets of fourteen generations. Mary is found pregnant before
her marriage to Joseph is consummated; Joseph, intending to quietly end the engagement, is visited
by an angel in a dream who tells him the child is "of the Holy Spirit" and instructs him to name
the child Jesus "for he will save his people from their sins." Matthew states this fulfills a
prophecy spoken through Isaiah about a virgin bearing a son named Immanuel.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 1:1 (WEB): "The book of the genealogy of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of
  Abraham."
- Matthew 1:17 (WEB): "So all the generations from Abraham to David are fourteen generations; from
  David to the exile to Babylon fourteen generations; and from the carrying away to Babylon to the
  Christ, fourteen generations."
- Matthew 1:20-21 (WEB): "But when he thought about these things, behold, an angel of the Lord
  appeared to him in a dream, saying, 'Joseph, son of David, don't be afraid to take to yourself

### 2. The Magi, the Flight to Egypt, and the Return to Nazareth (Matthew 2:1-23)
Wise men from the east follow a star to Jerusalem seeking "he who is born King of the Jews";
Herod, threatened, learns from his own priests and scribes that the Messiah is prophesied to be
born in Bethlehem, and secretly plots to find and kill the child. The magi worship Jesus and are
warned in a dream not to return to Herod. Warned in his own dream, Joseph flees with Mary and Jesus
to Egypt; Herod, enraged, orders the killing of Bethlehem's young boys. After Herod's death, the
family returns, settling in Nazareth. Matthew states three more fulfillment quotations across this
chapter.

### 3. John the Baptist and Jesus's Baptism (Matthew 3:1-17)
John the Baptist preaches repentance in the wilderness, quoting Isaiah, and baptizes crowds in the
Jordan, warning religious leaders not to presume on their ancestry. Jesus comes to be baptized by
John; John objects that he should be baptized by Jesus instead, but Jesus insists it is fitting "to
fulfill all righteousness." At the baptism, the Spirit descends like a dove and a voice from heaven
declares Jesus "my beloved Son."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 3:1-2 (WEB): "In those days, John the Baptizer came, preaching in the wilderness of
  Judea, saying, 'Repent, for the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand!'"
- Matthew 3:13-15 (WEB): "Then Jesus came from Galilee to the Jordan to John, to be
  baptized by him. But John would have hindered him, saying, 'I need to be baptized by you, and you

  the heavens were opened to him. He saw the Spirit of God descending as a dove, and coming on him.
  Behold, a voice out of the heavens said, 'This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased.'"

### 4. The Temptation in the Wilderness (Matthew 4:1-11)
Led by the Spirit into the wilderness, Jesus fasts forty days and is tempted three times by the
devil — to turn stones to bread, to throw himself from the temple to test God's protection, and to
receive the kingdoms of the world in exchange for worship. Jesus answers each temptation by quoting
Deuteronomy; the devil departs and angels attend him.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 4:1-2 (WEB): "Then Jesus was led up by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by
  the devil. When he had fasted forty days and forty nights, he was hungry afterward."
- Matthew 4:4 (WEB): "But he answered, 'It is written, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by
  every word that proceeds out of God's mouth."'"
- Matthew 4:10 (WEB): "Then Jesus said to him, 'Get behind me, Satan! For it is written, "You shall
  worship the Lord your God, and you shall serve him only."'"
- Matthew 4:11 (WEB): "Then the devil left him, and behold, angels came and served him."

### 5. Calling the First Disciples and the Dawn of Galilean Ministry (Matthew 4:12-25)

Sea of Galilee, he calls two pairs of fishermen brothers (Simon Peter and Andrew; James and John)
to follow him; they leave their nets immediately. His teaching, preaching, and healing draw great
crowds from across the region.

### 6. The Sermon on the Mount I: The Beatitudes, Salt and Light (Matthew 5:1-16)
Seeing the crowds, Jesus goes up a mountain and teaches his disciples, opening with the nine
Beatitudes — blessings pronounced on the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the gentle, those who
hunger for righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, the peacemakers, and the persecuted —
then tells the disciples they are "the salt of the earth" and "the light of the world."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 5:3-5 (WEB): "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven.

- Matthew 5:6-9 (WEB): "Blessed are those who hunger and thirst for righteousness, for they shall

### 7. The Sermon on the Mount II: Fulfilling the Law and the Antitheses (Matthew 5:17-48)
Jesus states he came not to abolish the Law and the Prophets but to fulfill them, and that his
followers' righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees. He then gives a series of
contrasts ("You have heard that it was said... but I tell you"), intensifying the Law's demands
beyond the outward act to the inward disposition — anger as much as murder, lust as much as
adultery — and closes with the command to love one's enemies.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 5:17 (WEB): "Don't think that I came to destroy the law or the prophets. I didn't come to
  destroy, but to fulfill."
- Matthew 5:21-22 (WEB, trimmed): "You have heard that it was said to the ancient ones, 'You shall
  not murder... But I tell you that everyone who is angry with his brother without a cause will be
  in danger of the judgment."
- Matthew 5:43-45 (WEB, trimmed): "You have heard that it was said, 'You shall love your neighbor
  and hate your enemy.' But I tell you, love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to
  those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you, that you may be
  children of your Father who is in heaven."

### 8. The Sermon on the Mount III: The Lord's Prayer and Anxiety (Matthew 6:9-13, 25-34)
Jesus teaches the disciples a model prayer — addressing God as Father, asking for daily provision,
forgiveness "as we also forgive our debtors," and deliverance from temptation — then teaches them
not to be anxious about food or clothing, pointing to the birds and the lilies, and to "seek first
God's Kingdom and his righteousness."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 6:9-13 (WEB): "Pray like this: 'Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy. Let
  your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven. Give us today our daily

  deliver us from the evil one. For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen.'"

- Matthew 6:26 (WEB): "See the birds of the sky, that they don't sow, neither do they reap, nor

- Matthew 6:33 (WEB): "But seek first God's Kingdom and his righteousness; and all these things will
  be given to you as well."

### 9. The Sermon on the Mount IV: The Golden Rule and the Two Builders (Matthew 7:1-29)
Jesus warns against judging others hypocritically, gives the Golden Rule ("whatever you desire for
men to do to you, you shall also do to them"), warns of false prophets known "by their fruits," and
closes the sermon with the parable of the wise man who built his house on rock and the foolish man
who built on sand. The crowds are astonished, "for he taught them with authority, and not like the
scribes."

**Challenge-ready verses:**

- Matthew 27:50-51 (WEB): "Jesus cried again with a loud voice, and yielded up his spirit. Behold,
  the veil of the temple was torn in two from the top to the bottom. The earth quaked and the rocks
  were split."
- Matthew 27:54 (WEB): "Now the centurion and those who were with him watching Jesus, when they saw
  the earthquake and the things that were done, were terrified, saying, 'Truly this was the Son of
  God!'"

and on our children!") is deliberately excluded entirely from this brief — not narrowly bounded,
excluded — see Contested Territory.*

### 18. The Resurrection and the Great Commission (Matthew 28:1-20)
On the third day, an angel rolls away the stone from the tomb and tells the women Jesus is not
there, "for he has risen, just like he said." Jesus himself meets them on the way. The eleven
disciples meet the risen Jesus on a mountain in Galilee; some worship him, some doubt. Jesus
declares "all authority" is his and commissions the disciples to make disciples of all nations,
"baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," promising to be
with them "always, even to the end of the age" — the book's closing words.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Matthew 28:5-6 (WEB): "The angel answered the women, 'Don't be afraid, for I know that you seek
  Jesus, who has been crucified. He is not here, for he has risen, just like he said. Come, see the
  place where the Lord was lying.'"
- Matthew 28:9 (WEB): "As they went to tell his disciples, behold, Jesus met them, saying,
  'Rejoice!' They came and took hold of his feet, and worshiped him."
- Matthew 28:18-19 (WEB): "Jesus came to them and spoke to them, saying, 'All authority has been
  given to me in heaven and on earth. Go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the
  name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.'" *(Sacramental theology — see Contested
  Territory. Recall-only format for the baptismal formula clause.)*
- Matthew 28:20 (WEB): "teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you. Behold, I am with
  you always, even to the end of the age."

### 1. The Fulfillment-Quotation Formula (Matthew 1:22-23, 2:15, 2:17-18, 2:23, 4:14-16, 8:17,
21:4-5) — the genuinely new category specific to Matthew, per this assignment's framing

Matthew's text itself repeatedly asserts that an event in Jesus's life "fulfilled" a specific
Hebrew Scripture passage. This is Matthew's own interpretive claim, made by the text — reporting
that Matthew's text makes this claim is in-bounds narrative fact, the same way reporting that
Genesis 3:15 exists and says what it says is in-bounds. **What is out-of-bounds is a challenge item
that requires the player to affirm, as settled fact independent of the text, that the underlying OT
passage "really was" a prediction of Jesus** — because in every case checked below, the OT passage's
own plain original-context meaning is either disputed or plainly about something else, and this
project has already made a deliberate choice on the OT side not to grade that question:

- **Matthew 1:22-23 quotes what Matthew's text calls a virgin-birth/Immanuel prophecy.** This
  project's Isaiah brief (per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §6's stated precedent, "Isaiah 7:14 excluded
  because of Matthew 1:23's use of it, not because of anything contested within Isaiah itself")
  already excluded Isaiah 7:14 from Isaiah's own challenge content for exactly this reason. This
  brief does not walk that back. **Bounding: Matthew 1:22-23 is used in recall/fill-in-blank format
  only, testing recall of what Matthew's own text states and quotes — never a multiple-choice item
  asking whether Isaiah 7:14 "really" predicted this, what Isaiah 7:14 "actually" refers to, or
  requiring the player to affirm the fulfillment claim as true independent of Matthew's own
  assertion of it.**
- **Matthew 2:15 quotes Hosea 11:1 ("Out of Egypt I called my son").** Hosea's own brief
  (`docs/content/hosea.md`, Contested Territory §"Hosea 11:1 and Matthew 2:15") already confirmed,
  with citations, that Hosea 11:1 in its own context plainly refers to Israel's exodus from Egypt
  (confirmed by 11:2-4's continued national-collective language), and that Matthew's application to
  Jesus is understood even within mainstream Christian scholarship as *typological*, not a direct
  verbal prediction — a distinction requiring careful handling to avoid over-claiming (see
  [The Gospel Coalition, "What's Christ Got to Do with Hosea 11:1? Getting a Grip on
  Typology"](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/article/whats-christ-got-to-do-with-hosea-111-getting-a-grip-on-typology/)).
  **Bounding, matching the Hosea brief's own stated principle exactly: Matthew 2:15 is used in
  recall/fill-in-blank format only, testing recall that Matthew's text states this fulfillment claim
  and quotes this line — never a multiple-choice item asking what the Hosea passage "really" means
  or refers to.** This is not walking back Hosea's OT-side exclusion; it is the same principle
  applied from the other direction, reporting Matthew's claim as Matthew's claim.
- **Matthew 2:17-18, 2:23, 4:14-16, 8:17, and 21:4-5** are the remaining formula quotations selected
  for this brief (of Jeremiah/Rachel weeping, an unattributed "Nazarene" prophecy whose exact OT
  source is itself a matter of scholarly debate, Isaiah's "Galilee of the Gentiles," Isaiah 53:4's
  suffering-servant language applied to physical healing rather than to the crucifixion, and
  Zechariah 9:9's humble king riding a donkey). None of these carry quite the same doctrinal weight
  as the virgin-birth or "out of Egypt" citations, but the same structural principle applies
  uniformly rather than case-by-case, so the same bounding is used for consistency and to avoid an
  arbitrary-seeming distinction: **all seven fulfillment-formula verses in this brief are
  recall/fill-in-blank format only.**
- **General rule for any future challenge-authoring pass:** a fulfillment-formula verse may be used
  to test "does Matthew's text state this quotation fulfills a named prophet's words, and what does
  Matthew's text quote" — never "was the OT passage really about Jesus," "what does the OT passage
  mean in its own right," or any item requiring the player to resolve the direct-prediction vs.
  typological-pattern vs. other-framework debate that even mainstream Christian scholarship treats
  as requiring care.

### 2. Soteriology and the Law (Matthew 5:17-20)
Jesus's statement that he came to "fulfill," not "destroy," the Law, and that a disciple's
righteousness must "exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees," sits at the center of Protestant/
Catholic differences on how grace, the Law, and good works relate to salvation. Catholic teaching
reads the "surpassing righteousness" of 5:20 as the fruit of sanctifying grace, a participation in
the divine nature; Protestant traditions (following an Augustinian moral/civil/ceremonial division
of the Law that both traditions actually share as a starting point) diverge sharply on how directly
that righteousness contributes to justification versus flows from it. See
[The Gospel Coalition, "Jesus and the law: an exegesis on Matthew
5:17-20"](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/themelios/article/jesus-and-the-law-an-exegesis-on-matthew-517-20/).
**Bounding: this brief uses 5:17 only as direct-quote recall (what Jesus states he came to do) and
does not select 5:20 itself for challenge content — no challenge item asks what "fulfilling the
law" means for how a person is saved, or grades any faith-vs-works position.**

### 3. Ecclesiology: "The Keys of the Kingdom" and "On This Rock" (Matthew 16:18-19)
This is, per multiple sources, the single most contested Catholic/Protestant/Orthodox fault line in
the entire New Testament regarding church authority. Confirmed via
[The Gospel Coalition, "What Does 'This Rock' Refer to in Matthew
16:18?"](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/article/what-does-this-rock-refer-to-matthew-1618/) and
[Catholic Answers, "Matthew 16 and a Parallel for
Peter"](https://www.catholic.com/magazine/online-edition/matthew-16-and-a-parallel-for-peter):
- **Catholic tradition** reads "this rock" as Peter himself, founding the papacy and apostolic
  succession.
- **Protestant traditions** offer two competing readings — Jesus himself as the rock (based on the
  Greek wordplay between *Petros*, "Peter"/small stone, and *petra*, "rock"), or Peter's confession
  of faith as the rock, not Peter as a person.
- **Orthodox tradition** accepts Rome/Peter as historically "first among equals" but rejects the
  Catholic doctrine of papal supremacy built on this verse.

This is a live, centuries-old, unresolved disagreement bearing directly on what a "correct answer"
about this verse's meaning would be. **Bounding: Matthew 16:18-19 is used in recall/fill-in-blank
format only, testing recall of the verbatim text — never a multiple-choice item asking what "this
rock" refers to, who holds "the keys," or what "binding and loosing" grants any office or
institution.**

### 4. General Church-Order Material (Matthew 18:15-20)
The step-by-step discipline procedure and "where two or three are gathered" both touch broader
ecclesiology (how church authority and gathering work) but with less denominationally sharp
disagreement than 16:18-19 specifically — Matthew 18 describes a *process* rather than asserting
Peter/papal authority. **Bounding: used at the narrative-fact level (the steps the text describes,
Peter's question, the numerical answer given) — no challenge item asks what kind of church
governance structure (congregational, presbyterian, episcopal) this passage requires or implies.**

### 5. Sacramental Theology: The Last Supper and the Baptismal Formula (Matthew 26:26-28, 28:19)
Two of the New Testament's clearest institution narratives for what different traditions call the
two (Protestant) or seven (Catholic/Orthodox, of which these are two) sacraments/ordinances:
- **The Lord's Supper/Eucharist (26:26-28).** Confirmed via
  [Catholic Answers' Bible Navigator on Matthew
  26:26-28](https://www.catholic.com/bible-navigator/real-presence/matthew2626-28), the [OPC's "Meaning
  of 'Real Presence' in the Lord's Supper"](https://www.opc.org/qa.html?question_id=332), and the
  [Wikipedia summary of Lord's Supper views in Reformed
  theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord%27s_Supper_in_Reformed_theology): Catholic teaching
  holds transubstantiation (the elements become Christ's body and blood); Lutheran teaching holds
  Christ present "in, with, and under" the elements without the elements changing; Reformed/Calvinist
  teaching holds a "spiritual real presence" received by faith, not orally; Zwinglian/most Baptist
  and Evangelical traditions hold a memorial view (purely symbolic); Orthodox teaching affirms a real
  presence while declining to explain the change philosophically. Five genuinely distinct positions
  across mainstream traditions on the same verse.
- **The baptismal formula (28:19).** Confirmed via multiple sources (see
  [GotQuestions, "What does the Bible say about infant
  baptism?"](https://www.gotquestions.org/infant-baptism.html) and
  [OrthodoxWiki, "Infant
  baptism"](https://orthodoxwiki.org/Infant_baptism)) that this verse anchors both the
  infant-baptism/paedobaptist position (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican, Presbyterian,
  Reformed, Methodist) and the believer's-baptism/credobaptist position (Baptist, most
  non-denominational Evangelical, Anabaptist-descended traditions), as well as disputes over mode
  (immersion vs. pouring/sprinkling) and whether baptism itself confers regeneration or symbolizes
  a prior conversion.

**Bounding, for both: this brief narrates the institution narratives (what Jesus did and said) and
uses the verses in recall/fill-in-blank format only — no challenge item asks what the bread and cup
"become" or accomplish, what baptism "does" spiritually, who is a valid recipient, or what mode is
correct.**

### 6. Atonement Theory (Matthew 26:28, and the crucifixion narrative generally)
"My blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins" is exactly
the kind of language different atonement theories (penal substitution, Christus Victor,
moral-influence, ransom) each claim as their strongest textual support, per
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's framing. **Bounding: the crucifixion and its stated purpose ("for the
remission of sins," per the text's own words) are narrated and quoted; no challenge item asks *how*
or *why* the crucifixion accomplishes forgiveness, or which theory of atonement is correct.**

### 7. Textual-Criticism Note: The Doxology of the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:13)
Independent of any doctrinal question, this is a genuine manuscript-tradition issue of the same kind
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7 flags for Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11, and 1 John 5:7-8. Confirmed via
multiple sources (see the [Wikipedia summary, "Matthew
6:13"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_6:13) and
[KJV Today's "Is the Doxology of the Lord's Prayer in Matthew 6:13 a late
addition?"](https://sites.google.com/site/kjvtoday/home/is-the-doxology-to-the-lords-prayer-in-matthew-613-a-late-addition))
that the closing line "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen." is absent
from the earliest and most reliable Greek manuscripts (Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus, both
4th-century), appears in at least ten different early forms before standardizing, and is present in
over 98% of later (mostly Byzantine-tradition) manuscripts — a textbook example of a well-attested
liturgical addition versus an earliest-manuscript reading. **WEB, like the KJV and NKJV, follows the
majority/Byzantine textual tradition here and includes the doxology**, unlike modern
critical-text-based translations (NIV, ESV, NRSV, NLT), which omit it or footnote it. Since this
project's translation choice (WEB) is fixed by Open Decision #1, this brief includes the doxology as
WEB actually prints it, with this note on the record — consistent with the project's practice on the
three other known NT textual-criticism cases (verify the printed text, note the issue, don't silently
resolve it).

### 8. Excluded Entirely: Matthew 27:24-25, Pilate's Handwashing and the Crowd's Response
"His blood be on us, and on our children" — the crowd's response after Pilate declares himself
innocent of Jesus's blood — is excluded entirely from this brief's challenge-ready content, not
narrowly bounded. This is not treated as ordinary contested theological territory but as a
content-integrity exclusion given documented real-world harm: confirmed via
[CSBV Bristol, "'On us and on our children': Repenting of the harm caused by Matthew
27:25"](https://www.csbvbristol.org.uk/2023/03/20/repenting-of-the-harm/) and multiple other sources
that this verse has the longest and most severe documented history of any New Testament verse being
used to justify anti-Jewish persecution and violence, from the early church fathers through the
Middle Ages and into the modern era, and that mainstream churches (e.g. the Church of England's
"God's Unfailing Word") have formally acknowledged and repented of this history. The narrative
context (Pilate offers to release either Barabbas or Jesus; the crowd, incited by the chief priests,
calls for Barabbas; Pilate washes his hands) is narrated in Scene 17 above without this specific
line. **No challenge item in this brief uses Matthew 27:24-25 as challenge-ready text, in any
format.**

### 9. Excluded Entirely: Matthew 8:11-12, "The Children of the Kingdom"
"The children of the kingdom will be thrown out into the outer darkness," spoken immediately after
praising the centurion's faith, has a well-documented history (including in patristic sources such
as Augustine, per [BibleHub's commentary aggregation on Matthew
8:12](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/matthew/8-12.htm)) of being read as asserting ethnic Israel's
wholesale replacement by Gentile believers — a supersessionist reading with the same real-world harm
profile as item 8 above, though with less documented severity. Other commentators read "children of
the kingdom" non-ethnically (as anyone presuming on inherited status rather than genuine faith,
Jewish or Christian). Given the live disagreement and the harm profile of the ethnic-replacement
reading, **this brief does not select Matthew 8:11-12 for challenge content at all** — the
centurion's-faith scene (Scene 10) draws only from 8:2-3, 8:8, and 8:16-17.

### 10. Excluded Entirely: The Olivet Discourse and Eschatological Material (Matthew 24-25)
Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's instruction that eschatology is "the single most fragmented
interpretive territory in all of Christian theology" and that Revelation should get the most
conservative posture "when in doubt... exclude rather than narrowly bound" — this brief extends the
same conservative posture to Matthew's Olivet Discourse, which contains comparably contested
material: the "abomination of desolation," signs of the end of the age, "this generation will not
pass away," the parable of the ten virgins, and the sheep-and-goats judgment scene. These sit
squarely inside the pre-/mid-/post-tribulation, premillennial/amillennial/postmillennial, and
preterist/futurist debates. **No scene in this brief is drawn from Matthew 24-25, and no verse from
these chapters is challenge-ready content.** This is a deliberate scope exclusion, not an oversight
— Matthew's narrative arc is complete without it (the Triumphal Entry leads directly into the Last
Supper in this brief's structure), the same way this project has excluded other doctrinally
saturated material outright elsewhere (e.g. Ezekiel 16/23, per the Hosea brief's own citation of that
precedent).

### 11. Not Selected: Genealogy Harmonization (Matthew 1 vs. Luke 3)
Matthew's genealogy (through Joseph, via Solomon) and Luke's genealogy (through a different line, via
Nathan) differ in ways that have generated a long history of harmonization proposals among
mainstream, orthodox commentators (e.g., one tracing Joseph's legal line and the other Mary's blood
line). This is a historicity/harmonization question of the same kind this project has already
treated as out of scope for grading (parallel to the Ezekiel 4:4-6 day-count precedent cited in the
Hosea review) rather than a live doctrinal fault line. **No challenge item in this brief requires
reconciling Matthew's and Luke's genealogies or asserts a specific harmonization is correct** — 1:1
and 1:17 are used only for Matthew's own stated genealogical structure (fourteen generations x
three), not as a claim about historical reconstruction.

### 12. Categories from the Style Guide's NT Addendum Confirmed Not Applicable to This Brief's
Selected Content

  are outside Matthew; no selected scene in this brief touches this category.
- **Gender-role/church-office passages in the classic sense (1 Timothy 2/3, Titus 1, 1 Corinthians
  11/14)** — these are Pauline/General Epistle texts, not present in Matthew. Matthew's own
  church-order material (item 4 above, 18:15-20) touches general ecclesiology but not gender roles

- **Predestination/free will (the Romans 9 fault line)** — not directly present in any scene selected

- **Known NT textual-criticism cases (Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11, 1 John 5:7-8)** — none of these
  are in Matthew; item 7 above (the Lord's Prayer doxology) is Matthew's own comparable case,
  surfaced independently during this review process.
