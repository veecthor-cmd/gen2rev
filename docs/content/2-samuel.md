## Sensitivity note (read before the scene below)

2 Samuel 11 — David's adultery with Bathsheba and the killing of her husband Uriah — is a real,
central turning point in David's story and in the book's own narrative logic (it directly causes
Nathan's confrontation, David's repentance, and the "sword will never depart from your house"
consequence that sets up Absalom's rebellion, this brief's next scene). It is also sexual content
and murder, in a general-audience/Disciple-tier game. This brief's decision, stated explicitly
rather than silently: **include the turning point as a pivotal scene, but bound the
challenge-ready verses to Nathan's confrontation and David's confession (2 Sam 12:1, 12:7, 12:13)
— narrowly factual, non-graphic — and do not select any verse from chapter 11 itself (the
encounter, the pregnancy, or the killing) as challenge-ready text.** The scene description below
states what happened in plain, non-graphic narrative language (consistent with how the text itself
narrates it, without invented detail) so the scene is not silently erased, but no challenge item
requires a player to read, recall, or fill in any verse depicting the act itself. This bounding
decision is called out explicitly for the theological reviewer to evaluate, per the assignment's
instruction not to resolve it unreviewed.

## Setting / Era

2 Samuel opens with Saul's death (concluding 1 Samuel) and covers David's reign as king: first over
Judah, then over all Israel; his establishment of Jerusalem as capital and bringing the ark there;
Yahweh's covenant promise of an enduring dynasty (2 Samuel 7); his sin with Bathsheba and its
consequences; and the unraveling of his household, climaxing in his son Absalom's rebellion and
death. The book ends with David's kingdom intact but scarred, setting up 1 Kings' account of his
final days and Solomon's succession.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. David Becomes King Over All Israel (2 Samuel 5)
After years of civil conflict following Saul's death, the elders of all the tribes of Israel come
to David at Hebron and anoint him king over the whole nation, not just Judah.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Samuel 5:1 (WEB): "Then all the tribes of Israel came to David at Hebron and spoke, saying,
  'Behold, we are your bone and your flesh.'"
- 2 Samuel 5:3 (WEB): "So all the elders of Israel came to the king to Hebron, and King David made
  a covenant with them in Hebron before Yahweh; and they anointed David king over Israel."
- 2 Samuel 5:4 (WEB): "David was thirty years old when he began to reign, and he reigned forty
  years."

### 2. The Ark Brought to Jerusalem (2 Samuel 6)
David brings the ark of the covenant to Jerusalem, dancing before Yahweh with all his might as it
enters the city amid celebration.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Samuel 6:12 (WEB): "So David went and brought up God's ark from the house of Obed-Edom into
  David's city with joy."
- 2 Samuel 6:14 (WEB): "David danced before Yahweh with all his might; and David was clothed in a
  linen ephod."
- 2 Samuel 6:15 (WEB): "So David and all the house of Israel brought up Yahweh's ark with shouting
  and with the sound of the trumpet."

### 3. The Davidic Covenant (2 Samuel 7)
When David proposes building a permanent house (temple) for Yahweh, Yahweh instead promises David,
through the prophet Nathan, an enduring dynasty — a promise referenced repeatedly across the rest
of the Hebrew Bible.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Samuel 7:12 (WEB): "When your days are fulfilled and you sleep with your fathers, I will set up
  your offspring after you, who will proceed out of your body, and I will establish his kingdom."
- 2 Samuel 7:13 (WEB): "He will build a house for my name, and I will establish the throne of his
  kingdom forever."
- 2 Samuel 7:16 (WEB): "Your house and your kingdom will be made sure forever before you. Your
  throne will be established forever."

### 4. Nathan Confronts David; David's Confession (2 Samuel 11-12)
David commits adultery with Bathsheba and arranges the death of her husband Uriah in battle to
conceal it. The prophet Nathan confronts David with a parable about a rich man who steals a poor
man's only lamb, then reveals David is the man in the story; David confesses and is told his sin
is forgiven, though consequences will follow. **See Sensitivity note above** for how this scene's
challenge content is bounded.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Samuel 12:1 (WEB): "Yahweh sent Nathan to David. He came to him, and said to him, 'There were
  two men in one city: the one rich, and the other poor.'"
- 2 Samuel 12:7 (WEB): "Nathan said to David, 'You are the man! This is what Yahweh, the God of
  Israel, says: I anointed you king over Israel, and I delivered you out of the hand of Saul.'"
- 2 Samuel 12:13 (WEB): "David said to Nathan, 'I have sinned against Yahweh.' Nathan said to
  David, 'Yahweh also has put away your sin. You will not die.'"

### 5. Absalom's Rebellion (2 Samuel 15, 18)
David's son Absalom wins the loyalty of the people over years and leads an open rebellion against
his father, forcing David to flee Jerusalem. The rebellion ends in Absalom's death; David, though
his throne is secured, grieves his son deeply.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Samuel 15:6 (WEB): "Absalom did this sort of thing to all Israel who came to the king for
  judgment. So Absalom stole the hearts of the men of Israel."
- 2 Samuel 15:14 (WEB): "David said to all his servants who were with him at Jerusalem, 'Arise!
  Let's flee, or else none of us will escape from Absalom. Hurry to depart, lest he overtake us
  quickly and bring down evil on us, and strike the city with the edge of the sword.'"
- 2 Samuel 18:33 (WEB): "The king was much moved, and went up to the room over the gate and wept.
  As he went, he said, 'My son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! I wish I had died instead of you,
  Absalom, my son, my son!'"

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **2 Samuel 7, the Davidic covenant — messianic reading.** This is likely the single most
  theologically load-bearing passage in the MVP's History-book worlds. Christian tradition reads
  "his kingdom forever" / "your throne will be established forever" as ultimately pointing to
  Jesus as the eternal Davidic heir (a messianic/typological reading built on in the New Testament,
  which is out of scope for this OT-only MVP). Jewish tradition reads the passage in its own
  context as a promise about David's dynastic line (fulfilled proximately in Solomon), without a
  Christian typological layer, though later Jewish messianic expectation is also connected to
  Davidic kingship in a different way. **The brief's challenge items test only the covenant's own
  stated terms — an offspring who will build a house, an established throne/kingdom "forever" —
  never which tradition's fulfillment reading is correct or whether it points to a specific later
  figure.** This needs explicit cross-tradition sourcing by the reviewer given how consequential
  the passage is. **Structural rule (added per theological review,
  `docs/reviews/2-samuel-review.md`): 2 Samuel 7:13 and 7:16 may only be used in recall/
  fill-in-blank format ("I will establish the throne of his ___ forever") — never as a
  multiple-choice item asking who "his kingdom" or "his throne" refers to** — so a future
  challenge-authoring pass can't accidentally build an item whose correct answer requires siding
  with one tradition's fulfillment reading over the other's.
- **2 Samuel 11-12, Bathsheba/Uriah/Nathan.** See the Sensitivity note above for the bounding
  decision. Beyond the sexual-content/violence sensitivity itself, there is also a live,
  values-laden question in modern discussion of this text about the power dynamic between a king
  and a subject's wife (whether "adultery" alone captures what happened, given the king's power to
  summon her) — this brief does not take a position on that question either; the challenge verses
  (12:1, 12:7, 12:13) describe only Nathan's confrontation and David's confession in the text's own
  words, not the initial encounter in chapter 11 at all.
- **2 Samuel 6, the ark narrative — Uzzah's death (not selected as challenge content).** The
  chapter this brief's Scene 2 draws from also includes Uzzah's death (6:6-7) when he touches the
  ark to steady it. This is a difficult passage about divine holiness that some traditions read
  very differently (a strict-holiness reading vs. framings that emphasize covenant/ritual
  procedure failures earlier in the chapter as the "real" cause). **This brief does not select any
  verse from 6:6-7 as challenge-ready and does not include Uzzah's death in the scene
  description**, scoping Scene 2 to the ark's arrival and celebration only. Flagging the exclusion
  explicitly, same approach as 1 Samuel 15's herem-command exclusion in this book's companion
  brief.
- **2 Samuel 15-18, Absalom's rebellion.** Touches on the theme of consequence within David's own
  household following chapter 12 (traditionally connected by many readers to Nathan's words in
  12:10-12, not selected as challenge-ready text here). The brief's challenge items test only the
  narrated events of the rebellion and David's grief (15:6, 15:14, 18:33) — never a causal/moral
  claim that the rebellion was divine punishment, which would cross from narrative fact into
  interpretation.
