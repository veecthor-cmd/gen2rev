## Setting / Era

Deuteronomy is set on the plains of Moab, at the very end of the 40 years of wilderness wandering,
just before Israel crosses the Jordan into Canaan. Structurally it is almost entirely Moses' own
farewell speech to the new generation — the one born after the Numbers 14 refusal, about to do what
their parents' generation would not. It restates the covenant and the law given at Sinai (recorded
in Exodus/Leviticus/Numbers), renews the covenant for this new generation, and ends with Moses'
death.

**This book's shape differs materially from Genesis's (and, as expected, from Leviticus's, being
written by a parallel agent in this same content pass).** Genesis is narrative-dense — many
discrete scenes across generations. Deuteronomy is discourse-heavy — one speaker, one setting, one
extended address, organized by sermon/legal-code structure rather than by a sequence of events. This
brief adapts the "pivotal scenes" concept accordingly: three of the five scenes below are pivotal
*passages* within the speech (the restated Ten Commandments, the Shema, the covenant-renewal
charge) rather than dramatic narrative beats, while two (Moses commissioning Joshua, Moses' death)
are genuine narrative scenes bookending the discourse.

**Narrative-bridge role (per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §3):** Deuteronomy 34 — Moses' death on
Mount Nebo — is deliberately included as a required pivotal scene, not an optional one. It is the
scene Joshua 1 assumes already happened; with Deuteronomy restored to the MVP list as World 5, this
book's closing scenes are what supply Joshua's narrative bridge, so World 6 no longer needs an
artificial recap.

### 2. The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4-9)
Immediately after the restated commandments, Moses gives Israel's central confession of faith and
the command to teach it continually to the next generation. This is the single most load-bearing
passage in the book for later Jewish liturgical and devotional practice.

### 3. The Covenant Renewed: Blessing, Curse, and "Choose Life" (Deuteronomy 11, 27-28, 30)
Moses sets the covenant before the people in binary terms — obedience brings blessing, rejection
brings curse — culminating in the direct charge to choose life. This brief uses the concise
framing verses (11:26-28, 30:15-20) as challenge-ready text rather than the lengthy, and in places
graphic, curse-list in Deuteronomy 28 itself (see Contested Territory).

**Challenge-ready verses:**

  if you listen to the commandments of Yahweh your God, which I command you today; and the curse,

### 5. The Death of Moses on Mount Nebo (Deuteronomy 34)
Moses climbs Mount Nebo, where Yahweh shows him the promised land he will not enter himself. He
dies there at 120, is buried by God in an unmarked grave, and is mourned 30 days. The book — and the
Pentateuch — closes noting no prophet like Moses has arisen since. This is the required narrative
bridge into World 6 (Joshua): Joshua 1 opens immediately after this scene.

**Challenge-ready verses:**

  Isaac, and to Jacob, saying, "I will give it to your offspring." I have caused you to see it with
  your eyes, but you shall not go over there.' So Moses the servant of Yahweh died there in the land
  of Moab, according to Yahweh's word."

**Numbering note:** the Ten Commandments' verse-by-verse enumeration (which commandment is "first,"
"second," etc.) differs across Jewish, Catholic, and most Protestant traditions, even though the
underlying WEB text of Deuteronomy 5 itself is identical across all of them. This brief's challenge
items reference verses by chapter:verse (e.g. "Deut 5:17"), never by ordinal commandment number
("the sixth commandment"), specifically to avoid taking a position on a numbering scheme one
tradition uses differently from another. See Contested Territory.

Boss battle (world 5 capstone): a mixed-format sequence spanning all 5 scenes, weighted toward
level 3–5 items, mirroring the Genesis brief's boss-battle structure.

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **Ten Commandments numbering (Deuteronomy 5, and its Exodus 20 parallel).** Jewish tradition,
  Catholic/Lutheran tradition, and most Reformed/Protestant traditions each number and divide the
  Ten Commandments differently (e.g. what Jewish tradition treats as the first "commandment" —
  "I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out" — most Protestant traditions treat as a preface, not
  a numbered commandment; Catholic/Lutheran tradition combines the "no other gods"/"no carved
  image" material differently from Reformed tradition). **This is a genuine, live cross-tradition
  difference over the same underlying text.** This brief's structural rule (verse-reference only,
  never ordinal-number labels) is meant to make the challenge format itself neutral to this
  disagreement — flagging for the reviewer to confirm that bounding actually holds, since it's
  functionally identical in kind to the Genesis 3:15/1:27 format restrictions the Genesis review
  already applied.
- **Deuteronomy 28's curse list — deliberately not used as challenge-ready text.** Deuteronomy 28
  (not quoted above) pairs an extended blessings section with an extended, and in its later verses
  genuinely graphic, curses section (siege conditions, disease, extreme deprivation). This brief
  uses only the compact framing verses (11:26-28, 30:19-20) that state the blessing/curse structure
  without the graphic content. Flagging this exclusion explicitly, same approach as the Numbers 25
  exclusion in `docs/content/numbers.md`, so it reads as a deliberate scope decision rather than an
  oversight.
- **The Shema's later liturgical role.** Deuteronomy 6:4-9 is not narratively contested — the text
  itself is not in dispute — but it carries enormous weight as the central declaration of Jewish
  monotheistic faith (recited daily in Jewish liturgy) and is also cited by Jesus in the Gospels
  (Mark 12:29-30, NT, out of MVP scope) as the greatest commandment. This brief's challenge items
  are scoped to recall/narrative-fact only (what the verse says, in what book/chapter it appears) —
  no challenge item asks about its liturgical use in any tradition or its NT citation, since both
  are outside this book's own text and outside MVP's OT-only scope.
- **The covenant-renewal ("choose life") framing and free will/covenant theology.** Deuteronomy
  30:15-20's stark "life and death, blessing and curse, choose life" framing touches directly on
  free will vs. covenant/election theology (Arminian/free-will readings vs. Reformed/predestination
  readings), an area with real, live intra-Christian-tradition disagreement. This brief's challenge
  items test only the verse's content and what Moses is recorded as saying, never a theological
  claim about human free will or divine sovereignty — same "stay narrative, don't grade the
  interpretation" bounding used elsewhere in this pipeline. **Structural rule (added per
  theological review, `docs/reviews/deuteronomy-review.md`): Deuteronomy 30:19-20 may be used only
  in recall/fill-in-blank or sequence-ordering format — never a multiple-choice "what does this
  verse mean/imply about free will" format** — the same prudence measure the Genesis brief applied
  to Genesis 3:15 and 1:27.
- **Moses' unmarked grave (Deuteronomy 34:6).** "No man knows where his tomb is to this day" (not
  used as challenge-ready text above, but part of the same scene) has drawn varied interpretive
  traditions about why (to prevent grave veneration/idolatry being one common reading, among
  others). This brief does not use that clause as challenge content and flags it here so the
  reviewer is aware it exists in the same passage as the verses that are used.
- **Violent conquest language in Moses' charge to Joshua (Deuteronomy 31:3-5, not fully quoted
  above).** The commissioning scene includes language about Yahweh destroying nations before
  Israel. This brief's chosen challenge verses (31:2-3, 7-8) keep to the succession/encouragement
  content ("be strong and courageous," Joshua taking over) and avoid the destruction-of-nations
  clauses as quotable text, consistent with the same narrative-only, non-graphic approach applied
  to Numbers' judgment scenes. Flagging since the full passage sits adjacent to just-war/ḥerem
  territory that `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 names as a live concern for Joshua (World 6) —
  this book's brief doesn't need to resolve that debate, but the reviewer should know the adjacency
  exists here too, one book earlier than Joshua.
