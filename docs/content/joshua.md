## Setting / Era

Joshua opens immediately after Moses' death, which closed World 5 (Deuteronomy). Joshua son of Nun,
already commissioned by Moses, leads the generation that grew up in the wilderness across the
Jordan into Canaan. The book covers three narrative movements: entry into the land (crossing the
Jordan, Jericho), the military campaigns that follow (southern and northern coalitions of Canaanite
kings), and the division of the conquered/allotted territory among the twelve tribes, closing with
Joshua's farewell. This is the first world in the "History" canon grouping (per
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §2) and the point where the story shifts from one family's story
(Genesis) and one nation's formation under law (Exodus–Deuteronomy) into a national history of
occupying and living in the land.

**A note on this brief's most sensitive material up front:** Joshua's conquest narrative includes
ḥerem ("devoted to destruction") warfare language — commands and reports of total destruction of
certain Canaanite cities and their populations. This is a real, live, unresolved ethical and
historical debate among serious scholars and religious traditions (see Contested Territory below).
This brief does not attempt to resolve that debate — it bounds challenge content to narrative fact
only and flags the material explicitly for the theological reviewer, per
`docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. Crossing the Jordan (Joshua 3-4)
With the ark of the covenant carried ahead by the priests, Israel crosses the Jordan River on dry
ground while it is at flood stage; twelve men, one per tribe, set up a memorial of twelve stones so
future generations will ask what they mean and be told the story.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Joshua 3:15-17 (WEB): "...the waters which came down from above stood, and rose up in one heap a
  great way off, at Adam, the city that is beside Zarethan... Then the people passed over near
  Jericho. The priests who bore the ark of Yahweh's covenant stood firm on dry ground in the middle
  of the Jordan; and all Israel crossed over on dry ground, until all the nation had passed
  completely over the Jordan."
- Joshua 4:6-7 (WEB): "...that this may be a sign among you, that when your children ask in the
  future, saying, What do you mean by these stones? then you shall tell them, Because the waters of
  the Jordan were cut off before the ark of Yahweh's covenant. When it crossed over the Jordan, the
  waters of the Jordan were cut off. These stones shall be for a memorial to the children of Israel
  forever."
- Joshua 4:22-24 (WEB, trimmed): "...you shall let your children know, saying, Israel came over
  this Jordan on dry land... that all the peoples of the earth may know that Yahweh's hand is
  mighty, and that you may fear Yahweh your God forever."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 3](https://ebible.org/web/JOS03.htm), [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 4](https://ebible.org/web/JOS04.htm).*

### 2. Rahab and the Spies (Joshua 2)
Joshua secretly sends two spies to Jericho. Rahab, a woman living in the city wall, hides them from
the king's men, tells them the city is afraid because of what they've heard Yahweh has done, and
asks them to spare her family in return. They swear an oath and tell her to mark her house with a
scarlet cord.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Joshua 2:1 (WEB): "Joshua the son of Nun secretly sent two men out of Shittim as spies, saying,
  'Go, view the land, including Jericho.'"
- Joshua 2:9-11 (WEB, trimmed): "I know that Yahweh has given you the land, and that the fear of you
  has fallen upon us... we have heard how Yahweh dried up the water of the Red Sea before you, when
  you came out of Egypt... our hearts melted, and there wasn't any more spirit in any man, because
  of you."
- Joshua 2:21 (WEB): "She said, 'Let it be as you have said.' She sent them away, and they departed.
  Then she tied the scarlet line in the window."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 2](https://ebible.org/web/JOS02.htm).*

### 3. The Fall of Jericho (Joshua 6)
Israel marches around the city once a day for six days, then seven times on the seventh day; at the
priests' trumpet blast and the people's shout, the wall falls, and the city is taken. Rahab and her
household, marked by the scarlet cord, are the one household spared.

**Challenge-ready verses** (recall/sequence content only — see note below on what is deliberately
excluded from this scene):
- Joshua 6:3-5 (WEB): "All of your men of war shall march around the city, going around the city
  once. You shall do this six days. Seven priests shall bear seven trumpets of rams' horns before
  the ark. On the seventh day, you shall march around the city seven times, and the priests shall
  blow the trumpets. It shall be that when they make a long blast with the ram's horn, and when you
  hear the sound of the trumpet, all the people shall shout with a great shout; then the city wall
  will fall down flat."
- Joshua 6:20 (WEB): "So the people shouted and the priests blew the trumpets. When the people heard
  the sound of the trumpet, the people shouted with a great shout, and the wall fell down flat, so
  that the people went up into the city, every man straight in front of him, and they took the
  city."
- Joshua 6:25 (WEB): "But Rahab the prostitute, her father's household, and all that she had, Joshua
  saved alive. She lives in the middle of Israel to this day, because she hid the messengers whom
  Joshua sent to spy out Jericho."

**Deliberately excluded from challenge-ready content:** Joshua 6:17 and 6:21 — the verses stating
the city and "all that was in it" were "devoted" to Yahweh and "utterly destroyed... both man and
woman, both young and old" — are part of this chapter's text but are **not** selected as
challenge-ready verses for this scene. They are addressed narratively (i.e., the brief does not
pretend they aren't in the text) in the Contested Territory section below, not turned into a
recall/quiz item, per the assignment's explicit instruction not to build graded content that puts a
player in the position of processing that language as a quiz answer.

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 6](https://ebible.org/web/JOS06.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Joshua 6](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua+6&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

### 4. The Conquest Campaigns (Joshua 10-11) — see Contested Territory, handled narrowly
Joshua leads Israel against a coalition of five southern Amorite kings (Joshua 10) and later a
northern coalition led by Jabin of Hazor (Joshua 11). The text reports Joshua "took all these kings
and their land at one time because Yahweh, the God of Israel, fought for Israel" (10:42) and
summarizes the campaign's territorial reach.

**Challenge-ready verses** (geographic/summary content only, recall format only — see restriction
note):
- Joshua 10:41-42 (WEB): "Joshua struck them from Kadesh Barnea even to Gaza, and all the country of
  Goshen, even to Gibeon. Joshua took all these kings and their land at one time because Yahweh, the
  God of Israel, fought for Israel."

**Deliberately excluded from challenge-ready content:** Joshua 10:40's clause describing that Joshua
"left no one remaining, but... utterly destroyed all that breathed" (the ḥerem/total-destruction
language) is **not** selected as challenge-ready verse text for the same reason as 6:17/6:21 above.
This scene exists in the brief — the conquest campaigns are pivotal to the book and to the "how
Israel came to occupy the land" thread the rest of the History worlds assume — but no challenge item
built from it may ask *why* the campaigns happened, *whether they were commanded/justified*, or
require the player to affirm or evaluate the ḥerem practice in any way. **Structural rule:** any
future challenge item drawing on this scene beyond the two geographic/summary verses above must be
recall/fill-in-blank format only, never multiple-choice framed around motive or justification, and
must be reviewed against this brief's Contested Territory section before authoring.

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 10](https://ebible.org/web/JOS10.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Joshua 10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Joshua+10&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

### 5. Dividing the Land (Joshua 14, 21:43-45)
Caleb, one of the two faithful spies from 45 years earlier, asks Joshua for the hill country
promised to him by Moses. The book's conquest section closes with a summary statement: Yahweh gave
Israel all the land he had promised their fathers, and none of it failed.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Joshua 14:10-12 (WEB, trimmed): "...now, behold, I am eighty-five years old, today. As yet I am
  as strong today as I was in the day that Moses sent me... Now therefore give me this hill
  country, of which Yahweh spoke in that day..."
- Joshua 21:43-45 (WEB): "So Yahweh gave to Israel all the land which he swore to give to their
  fathers. They possessed it, and lived in it. Yahweh gave them rest all around, according to all
  that he swore to their fathers. Not a man of all their enemies stood before them. Yahweh
  delivered all their enemies into their hand. Nothing failed of any good thing which Yahweh had
  spoken to the house of Israel. All came to pass."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 14](https://ebible.org/web/JOS14.htm), [ebible.org WEB, Joshua 21](https://ebible.org/web/JOS21.htm).*

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **Ḥerem / the conquest as "genocide" vs. "just war": the central, unresolved item in this brief.**
  Joshua 6:17, 6:21, and 10:40 (among others) describe total destruction of Jericho's and other
  cities' populations, framed in the text as commanded by Yahweh. This is not a settled question
  among mainstream scholars or traditions:
  - Jewish scholarship and popular treatment (My Jewish Learning) frames it plainly as a genuine
    moral problem: the text itself gives no case that the Canaanites had personally sinned against
    Israel — the stated rationale is religious (their gods), and terms like "war crime" and
    "ethnic cleansing" appear in serious discussion of the ḥerem law.
  - Evangelical apologetics (The Gospel Coalition/Themelios, "Helpful Distinction or Quarrel over
    Words?") documents active, ongoing disagreement even *within* evangelical scholarship over
    whether "genocide" is an apt or unfair term, whether the destruction language is ancient hyperbolic
    war rhetoric rather than literal reporting, and whether the episode is a unique,
    non-repeatable act tied to that specific land/time rather than a general model.
  - TheTorah.com's critical-scholarship treatment of Deuteronomy's ḥerem law describes it as
    "protecting Israel at the cost of its humanity" — i.e., treats the ethical problem as real and
    not fully resolved by any single reading.
  - There is no cross-tradition consensus on how to read this material, and the disagreement is not
    a minor interpretive nuance — it runs to whether the text describes something rightly called a
    war crime.
  This brief's response: exclude the destruction-language verses themselves from challenge-ready
  content (see Scenes 3 and 4 above), keep the surrounding narrative (crossing the river, Rahab,
  the trumpets/shout, the land division) fully in-bounds and challenge-ready. The `theological-
  reviewer` agent escalated Scene 4 rather than resolve it unilaterally; **Kachi Okere, acting as
  the named human theological reviewer, confirmed on 2026-08-08 that this exclusion-based bounding
  is sufficient** — Scene 4 stays in the brief, permanently limited to the two geographic/summary
  verses, with the destruction clauses permanently excluded. See `docs/reviews/joshua-review.md` for
  the reviewer's full findings and the addendum at the bottom of that file for this sign-off.
- **Historicity of the conquest.** Archaeological and historical-critical scholarship widely
  disputes whether the conquest happened as a rapid military campaign versus a longer, more gradual
  settlement process (parallel debate structure to Genesis's Flood-scope item). Same handling as
  Genesis: no challenge item asserts or requires a position on the historicity debate; challenges
  test only the narrative as told.
- **The land as promise, invoked in modern geopolitical contexts.** Joshua's land-grant theology
  (Yahweh giving Israel the land, e.g. 21:43-45) is sometimes invoked in present-day political
  debate unrelated to the book's own narrative content. Per the same prudence logic applied to
  Genesis 1:27, **structural rule: challenge items built from 21:43-45 must stay in
  recall/fill-in-blank or narrative-sequence format only — never a multiple-choice format framed
  around "what this means for [any present-day claim]."**
- **Rahab's introduction as "the prostitute" (Josh 2:1, 6:25).** Not a live cross-tradition dispute
  (all mainstream traditions read the Hebrew plainly), but flagged as a term that needs
  age-appropriate handling in UI copy/voice-over outside the challenge-item text itself — a
  frontend/design concern more than a theological one, noted here so it isn't lost.
