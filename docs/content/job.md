## Setting / Era

Job is set outside Israel's national story entirely — "the land of Uz," a wisdom-literature
setting with no named king, no exodus, no covenant history attached. Its era is deliberately
unanchored; the book gives no date and stands somewhat outside the Genesis-through-Chronicles
historical arc the first 17 worlds trace. As the first Wisdom/Poetry world, it also marks a
structural shift for the player: worlds 1-17 were narrative-driven, and Job (like Leviticus before
it, world 3) is **light narrative frame wrapped around non-narrative content** — but where
Leviticus wraps legal/ritual material, Job wraps roughly 40 chapters of poetic dialogue (Job's
three friends, then a fourth speaker Elihu, then God). Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §6, this
book follows Leviticus's precedent of adapting to pivotal *passages* rather than forcing a
narrative-scene structure onto material that isn't narrative.

**Structural choice for this brief:** the prologue (chs. 1-2) and epilogue (ch. 42) are genuine
narrative and are treated as **narrative frame scenes** below, exactly like any of worlds 1-17.
The poetic middle (chs. 3-37, the dialogue between Job and his three friends, plus Elihu's speech
in chs. 32-37) is **not** converted into pivotal passages or challenge content at all — see
Contested Territory for why. Only God's speeches from the whirlwind (chs. 38-41) are drawn from
for **pivotal passages**, alongside one standalone, famous quotation from the dialogue (Job
19:25-27) that stands on its own outside the friends' argument.

## Narrative Frame Scenes (3)

### 1. Job's Prosperity and the Test Begins (Job 1:1-3, 1:6-12)
Job is introduced as blameless, upright, and the wealthiest man in the region. In a scene set in
the heavenly court, "the Satan" appears among God's sons and questions whether Job's righteousness
would survive losing everything; Yahweh permits a test, with a limit ("only on himself don't
stretch out your hand").

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 1:1-3 (WEB): "There was a man in the land of Uz, whose name was Job. That man was blameless
  and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from evil. There were born to him seven
  sons and three daughters. His possessions also were seven thousand sheep, three thousand camels,
  five hundred yoke of oxen, five hundred female donkeys, and a very great household; so that this
  man was the greatest of all the children of the east."
- Job 1:8 (WEB): "Yahweh said to Satan, 'Have you considered my servant, Job? For there is no one
  like him on the earth, a blameless and an upright man, one who fears God, and turns away from
  evil.'"
- Job 1:12 (WEB): "Yahweh said to Satan, 'Behold, all that he has is in your power. Only on
  himself don't stretch out your hand.' So Satan went out from the presence of Yahweh."

*(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory: "The Satan" figure.)*

### 2. Job's Losses and His Response (Job 1:13-22, 2:7-10)
In a single day, raiders and a great wind take Job's livestock, servants, and all ten children.
Job tears his robe, shaves his head, and worships, declaring one of the book's most famous lines.
Later, struck with painful sores, he refuses his wife's suggestion to curse God and die.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 1:20-21 (WEB): "Then Job arose, and tore his robe, and shaved his head, and fell down on the
  ground, and worshiped. He said, 'Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked will I return
  there. Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away. Blessed be Yahweh's name.'"
- Job 1:22 (WEB): "In all this, Job didn't sin, nor charge God with wrongdoing."
- Job 2:9-10 (WEB): "Then his wife said to him, 'Do you still maintain your integrity? Renounce
  God, and die.' But he said to her, 'You speak as one of the foolish women would speak. What?
  Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?' In all this Job didn't
  sin with his lips."

### 3. Job's Response and Restoration (Job 42:1-8, 42:10-17)
After God's speeches from the whirlwind, Job answers briefly, then Yahweh rebukes Job's three
friends for their speech about him and restores Job's fortunes twofold.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 42:2-3 (WEB, trimmed): "'I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can
  be restrained... I have uttered that which I didn't understand, things too wonderful for me,
  which I didn't know.'"
- Job 42:5-6 (WEB): "'I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you.
  Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes.'" *(Handled per the format restriction
  below — see Contested Territory: Job 42:6.)*
- Job 42:7 (WEB, trimmed): "Yahweh said to Eliphaz the Temanite, 'My wrath is kindled against you,
  and against your two friends; for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my
  servant Job has.'"
- Job 42:10 (WEB): "Yahweh restored Job's prosperity when he prayed for his friends. Yahweh gave
  Job twice as much as he had before."
- Job 42:12 (WEB, trimmed): "So Yahweh blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning. He
  had fourteen thousand sheep, six thousand camels, one thousand yoke of oxen, and a thousand
  female donkeys."
- Job 42:16-17 (WEB): "After this Job lived one hundred forty years, and saw his sons, and his
  sons' sons, to four generations. So Job died, being old and full of days."

## Pivotal Passages from the Poetic Middle (4)

### 1. God's First Speech: The Foundations of the Earth (Job 38:1-7)
God answers Job "out of the whirlwind" — not with an explanation of Job's suffering, but with a
sequence of questions about the created order, opening with the earth's foundations.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 38:1-3 (WEB): "Then Yahweh answered Job out of the whirlwind, 'Who is this who darkens
  counsel by words without knowledge? Brace yourself like a man, for I will question you, then you
  answer me!'"
- Job 38:4-7 (WEB): "'Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if you
  have understanding. Who determined its measures, if you know? Or who stretched the line on it?
  What were its foundations fastened on? Or who laid its cornerstone, when the morning stars sang
  together, and all the sons of God shouted for joy?'"

### 2. God's Speech: The Sea, Death, and the Cosmos (Job 38:16-18, 38:31-33)
The speech continues through the sea's springs, the gates of death, the earth's width, and the
constellations — physical/creation imagery throughout, no doctrinal claim beyond "God made and
governs all this, and Job did not."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 38:16-17 (WEB): "'Have you entered into the springs of the sea? Or have you walked in the
  recesses of the deep? Have the gates of death been revealed to you? Or have you seen the gates
  of the shadow of death?'"
- Job 38:31-32 (WEB): "'Can you bind the cluster of the Pleiades, or loosen the cords of Orion? Can
  you lead the constellations out in their season? Or can you guide the Bear with her cubs?'"

### 3. God's Speech: The Warhorse (Job 39:19-25)
Still within the whirlwind speech, God turns to vivid animal imagery — here, the war-horse's
fearlessness in battle.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 39:19-20 (WEB): "'Have you given the horse might? Have you clothed his neck with a quivering
  mane? Have you made him to leap as a locust? The glory of his snorting is awesome.'"
- Job 39:22 (WEB): "'He mocks at fear, and is not dismayed, neither does he turn back from the
  sword.'"

### 4. Job's Hope: "I Know That My Redeemer Lives" (Job 19:25-27)
Mid-dialogue, in the middle of arguing with his friends, Job speaks one of the book's most quoted
lines — a declaration of hope that stands on its own as a passage, independent of the surrounding
argument's content.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Job 19:25 (WEB): "But as for me, I know that my Redeemer lives. In the end, he will stand upon
  the earth."
- Job 19:26-27 (WEB): "'After my skin is destroyed, then I will see God in my flesh, whom I, even
  I, will see on my side. My eyes will see, and not as a stranger.'" *(Handled per the format
  restriction below — see Contested Territory: Job 19:25-27.)*

## Difficulty Ladder

| Level | Type | Example (from this book) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Recall | Verse fill-in-blank | "Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has ___ away." (Job 1:21) → taken |
| 2 — Recall | Multiple choice, narrative fact | "What did Job's wife suggest he do?" → renounce God and die (Job 2:9) |
| 3 — Sequence | Order scenes within the book | Order the narrative beats (prosperous and blameless → loses everything in one day → afflicted with sores → restored twofold) |
| 4 — Sequence + recall | Order + attribute a quotation | Given three quotations (1:21, 38:4, 42:5-6), place them in the order they occur and match each to its reference |
| 5 — Application (narrative-fact only, not moral judgment) | Multiple choice on textual detail | "According to Job 42:12, how many sheep did Job have after his restoration — compared to Job 1:3?" → fourteen thousand, twice his original seven thousand (tests attentive reading/comparison of the text, not the meaning of the restoration) |

Boss battle (world 18 capstone): a mixed-format sequence spanning both the narrative frame and the
whirlwind-speech passages, weighted toward level 3-5 items. Job 1:21 and Job 19:25 (recall-format
only, per the restrictions below) are the two strongest single-line recall candidates for this
world, being among the most widely known verses in the book across traditions. Because roughly 40
of Job's 42 chapters are deliberately excluded from this brief (see Contested Territory), expect —
same as Leviticus — a higher proportion of narrative-frame and whirlwind-speech recall items
relative to sequence items, a legitimate consequence of the source material's shape.

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here.

- **The core assignment guardrail, restated as a design rule:** no challenge item in this world may
  ask a player to affirm or reject a position on *why God allows suffering*, or to judge *whether
  Job's friends' theology was right or wrong*. Every item below is scoped to narrative fact or
  direct verse recall only.

- **"The Satan" figure (Job 1:6-12).** Genuine cross-tradition divergence on who/what this figure
  is. Jewish tradition (e.g. Sefaria-sourced commentary) reads "ha-satan" as a heavenly
  prosecutor/accuser role — a subordinate member of the divine court carrying out an
  investigative/testing function under God's explicit limits, not the cosmic, independently evil
  Devil of later theology. Some Christian sources read the same figure as identical to "the devil"
  of the New Testament and later Christian doctrine. WEB's own English rendering capitalizes
  "Satan" without the definite article, which itself reflects one translation choice rather than a
  neutral rendering of the Hebrew "ha-satan." **This brief's bounding:** challenge items use only
  the WEB text's own wording and test narrative fact (what Satan says, what Yahweh permits and
  limits) — no item asks the player to identify what/who Satan theologically *is*, and no item
  frames this figure with any label ("the Devil," "a fallen angel," etc.) beyond what the WEB text
  itself uses ("Satan"). **Structural rule: this scene's items are recall/fill-in-blank or
  narrative-fact multiple choice about actions/dialogue only — never a multiple-choice item that
  asks "who/what is Satan" or similar identity/nature questions.**

- **Job 1:21, "Yahweh gave, and Yahweh has taken away."** Not itself doctrinally contested as a
  quotation, but it sits directly adjacent to the suffering question this brief is designed around.
  Recall-format only (fill-in-blank / cloze), consistent with how Genesis 3:15 and Leviticus 10:1-3
  were restricted for similar prudence reasons — no "what does this verse mean" multiple-choice
  framing.

- **Job 19:25-27, "I know that my Redeemer lives."** Confirmed genuine, deep cross-tradition
  divergence on meaning. Christian tradition has long read this as pointing toward bodily
  resurrection and, in some traditions, toward Christ as redeemer. Jewish and much critical
  scholarship reads the underlying Hebrew *go'el* (kinsman-redeemer) as most likely a hope for
  vindication of Job's name within his lifetime or immediately after death, not a resurrection
  doctrine — and scholarship broadly agrees the Hebrew itself is genuinely ambiguous on this point
  (see `docs/reviews/job-review.md` §3 for sources). **Structural rule: this passage may only be
  used in recall/fill-in-blank format (reciting the verbatim WEB text) — never a multiple-choice
  "what does Job mean by 'Redeemer'" or "who is the Redeemer" format**, so the item can never be
  read as taking a side on the resurrection/messianic question.

- **Job 42:6, "I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes."** The underlying Hebrew is genuinely
  contested at the translation level — scholars disagree on whether the verb rendered "abhor
  myself" has an implied object at all, whether Job is repenting of sin, recanting specific
  statements he made about God, or simply relenting/being comforted, given the prologue already
  establishes Job's innocence (see `docs/reviews/job-review.md` §3). **Structural rule: verbatim
  WEB recall/fill-in-blank format only — no item asks what Job means by this line or whether Job
  is confessing wrongdoing**, since the text's own translators disagree on that point before any
  theological reading is even applied.

- **Job 42:7, God's rebuke of the friends.** This is a stated textual fact, safe to include (Yahweh
  says he is angry with the friends "for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my
  servant Job has") — but per the core guardrail above, no challenge item asks *why* the friends'
  theology was wrong, what specifically they got wrong, or invites the player to evaluate their
  arguments. The item tests only that the text states this rebuke happened and to whom.

- **Deliberately excluded material: the entire dialogue and Elihu's speech (Job 3-37).** This is
  the largest exclusion of any book's brief so far — roughly 34 of the book's 42 chapters. These
  chapters are the friends' speeches (Eliphaz, Bildad, Zophar) arguing that Job's suffering must be
  deserved, Job's counter-arguments maintaining his innocence, and a fourth speaker (Elihu) offering
  yet another take before God speaks. No verse from these chapters is used as challenge-ready text
  anywhere in this brief, **not for sensitivity reasons but because there is no way to build a
  graded item from this material that doesn't require the player to affirm or reject a position on
  the suffering question** — which speaker is right, whether retribution theology holds, whether
  Job's protests of innocence are vindicated (that ambiguity is itself part of the book's design,
  and even scholars are divided on why exactly Job is vindicated in 42:7). Flagged explicitly, per
  the Leviticus precedent, so this exclusion reads as a considered scope decision rather than a
  gap.

- **Deliberately excluded: the explicit death-report verses (Job 1:18-19).** Scene 2 above
  narrates "loses everything in one day" and quotes Job's response (1:20-22) and the sores/wife
  exchange (2:9-10), but deliberately does **not** use Job 1:18-19 (the messenger's report that a
  great wind collapsed the house and killed all ten of Job's children) as challenge-ready text.
  Unlike Leviticus 10:1-2 (Nadab and Abihu), which was included with a structural pairing rule, this
  brief judges the direct death of ten named-by-implication children as a step further on the
  child-directed-content line and leaves it as unquoted narrative background only — the player
  encounters that this loss happened (via Job's response in 1:20-22, which presupposes it) without
  a graded item built from the report of the deaths themselves.

- **Deliberately excluded material: Behemoth and Leviathan (Job 40:15-41:34).** God's second
  speech turns to two creatures described in vivid, extended terms. Excluded for two independent
  reasons: (1) creature-identification is itself contested — some traditions read these as
  poetic/mythological figures representing untamable chaos, others (particularly within young-earth
  creationist apologetics) read Behemoth specifically as a literal, possibly dinosaur-like animal —
  a live, unresolved debate structurally similar to the creation-days/flood-scope exclusions already
  accepted in the Genesis and Leviticus briefs; (2) the descriptions include intense, borderline
  frightening imagery (breathing fire, in Leviathan's case) that raises the same child-directed
  content-appropriateness question flagged for Leviticus 10 and Leviticus 18/20. Both reasons
  independently support exclusion, so no verse from these chapters appears in this brief.

- **Why God answers with nature imagery instead of directly addressing Job's suffering (chs.
  38-41 as a whole).** This is itself a major interpretive question in Job scholarship — sovereignty
  emphasis, wisdom-literature humility, cosmic-order-vs-human-suffering framing, and others are all
  live readings. No challenge item in this brief asks *why* God answers this way or what the
  whirlwind speeches are "really" saying — items test only the content of the imagery itself
  (what God asks about, in the text's own words).
