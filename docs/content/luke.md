## Setting / Era

Luke is the longest of the four Gospels and, per its own opening (1:1-4), the most self-consciously
historiographical — addressed to "most excellent Theophilus," an orderly account compiled from
eyewitness testimony, so that its reader "may know the certainty concerning the things" he was
taught. It narrates the life of Jesus of Nazareth from before his birth (opening with the
priest Zechariah in the Jerusalem temple, under Roman client-king Herod the Great) through his
public ministry in Galilee and Judea, to his crucifixion in Jerusalem under the Roman governor
Pontius Pilate and, distinctively, a resurrection appearance that extends the narrative through the
day of the resurrection itself and into the road to Emmaus, ending with Jesus's departure ("carried
up into heaven," 24:51) — a scene Luke alone among the Gospels narrates in detail and then reopens
as the opening scene of his second volume, Acts (per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's wave grouping,
Acts is the next book in this project's NT sequence).

Luke is traditionally identified as a Gentile physician and companion of Paul (Colossians 4:14);
authorship, while widely held across mainstream traditions, is not itself graded content in this
brief — no challenge item asserts authorship as verse text or narrative fact beyond what the text
itself states (which is silent on the author's own name).

**What makes this brief distinctively Luke's Gospel, not a generic Gospel retelling:** Matthew,
Mark, and John are being authored in parallel by separate agents for worlds 40, 41, and 43 (per
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's Wave 1), so this brief deliberately weights toward what standard
Gospel scholarship identifies as unique to Luke's telling, rather than the beats every Gospel
shares:

- **By far the most detailed birth/infancy narrative of any Gospel** (Luke 1-2) — Zechariah and
  Elizabeth, Gabriel's annunciation to Mary, the Magnificat, the shepherds and the manger, Simeon
  and Anna, and the boy Jesus in the temple. Matthew has a birth narrative too (different material —
  the magi, Herod's slaughter of the infants, the flight to Egypt), but Luke's is longer, more
  personal in register, and built around named, ordinary people (a barren elderly priest's wife, a
  teenage betrothed woman, shepherds) rather than Matthew's political/court framing. This brief
  gives the infancy material six of its eighteen scenes — the single heaviest emphasis of any
  section.
- **Several of the most famous parables in the entire Bible, found only in Luke** — the Good
  Samaritan (10:25-37), the Prodigal Son (15:11-32), and the Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19-31) all
  appear in no other Gospel. These are load-bearing, not filler — this brief treats them as pivotal
  scenes on par with any narrative event.
- **A marked, sustained emphasis on the marginalized, the poor, women, and outsiders.** The
  Magnificat itself ("he has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly... he
  has sent the rich away empty," 1:52-53) states this as a theme in the text's own words before the
  narrative even begins. It continues through shepherds (low-status witnesses) at the birth, the
  Nazareth sermon's citation of Isaiah's "good news to the poor" (4:18), the Samaritan as the story's
  moral example rather than its villain (10:33-37, notable given first-century Jewish-Samaritan
  hostility — a point this brief treats as narrative fact, not a claim requiring outside context to
  grade), the tax collector Zacchaeus (19:1-10), and women named as Jesus's traveling companions and
  as the first witnesses to the empty tomb (24:1-11) whose report the male disciples initially
  dismissed as "nonsense" (24:11) — the text's own stated detail, not this brief's editorializing.
- **A distinctive resurrection account.** Luke's resurrection narrative (ch. 24) is built around the
  road to Emmaus (24:13-35), a scene found in no other Gospel, plus a more extended set of
  post-resurrection appearance and departure material than Mark's original ending provides.

## Pivotal Scenes (18)

### 1. Zechariah and Elizabeth: Gabriel's Annunciation to Zechariah (Luke 1:5-25)
An elderly, childless priest, Zechariah, is serving in the Jerusalem temple when the angel Gabriel
appears and announces that his wife Elizabeth will bear a son, John, who will "go before" the Lord
"in the spirit and power of Elijah." Zechariah doubts, given their age, and is struck mute until the
child is born. Elizabeth conceives.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 1:13-15 (WEB, trimmed): "But the angel said to him, 'Don't be afraid, Zacharias, because your
  request has been heard. Your wife, Elizabeth, will bear you a son, and you shall call his name
  John... For he will be great in the sight of the Lord... He will be filled with the Holy Spirit,
  even from his mother's womb.'"
- Luke 1:18 (WEB): "Zacharias said to the angel, 'How can I be sure of this? For I am an old man,
  and my wife is well advanced in years.'"
- Luke 1:24-25 (WEB): "After these days Elizabeth his wife conceived, and she hid herself five
  months, saying, 'Thus has the Lord done to me in the days in which he looked at me, to take away
  my reproach among men.'"

### 2. Gabriel's Annunciation to Mary (Luke 1:26-38)
Six months later, Gabriel appears to Mary, a virgin in Nazareth pledged to marry Joseph of David's
house, and announces she will bear a son, Jesus, who "will be great and will be called the Son of
the Most High." Mary asks how, "seeing I am a virgin"; Gabriel answers that the Holy Spirit will
come upon her. Mary consents. **This is the book's most doctrinally load-bearing scene — see
Contested Territory: The Virgin Birth below for how this brief bounds it.**

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 1:30-31 (WEB): "The angel said to her, 'Don't be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with
  God. Behold, you will conceive in your womb and give birth to a son, and shall name him
  "Jesus."'"
- Luke 1:32-33 (WEB): "He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High. The Lord God
  will give him the throne of his father David, and he will reign over the house of Jacob forever.
  There will be no end to his Kingdom." *(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested
  Territory: The Virgin Birth.)*
- Luke 1:34-35 (WEB): "Mary said to the angel, 'How can this be, seeing I am a virgin?' The angel
  answered her, 'The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow
  you. Therefore also the holy one who is born from you will be called the Son of God.'" *(Handled
  per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory: The Virgin Birth.)*
- Luke 1:38 (WEB): "Mary said, 'Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to
  your word.' Then the angel departed from her."

### 3. The Magnificat: Mary Visits Elizabeth (Luke 1:39-56)
Mary travels to visit Elizabeth; at her greeting, the unborn John "leaped" in Elizabeth's womb, and
Elizabeth, filled with the Holy Spirit, blesses Mary. Mary responds with an extended song of praise
(the "Magnificat"), celebrating God's mercy toward the lowly and hungry against the proud and rich —
the passage this brief's introduction points to as stating Luke's marginalized/outsider emphasis in
the text's own words.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 1:41-42 (WEB): "When Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leaped in her womb; and
  Elizabeth was filled with the Holy Spirit. She called out with a loud voice and said, 'Blessed are
  you among women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!'"
- Luke 1:46-49 (WEB): "Mary said, 'My soul magnifies the Lord. My spirit has rejoiced in God my
  Savior, for he has looked at the humble state of his servant. For behold, from now on, all
  generations will call me blessed. For he who is mighty has done great things for me. Holy is his
  name.'"
- Luke 1:52-53 (WEB): "He has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly. He has
  filled the hungry with good things. He has sent the rich away empty."

### 4. The Birth of Jesus: Shepherds and the Manger (Luke 2:1-20)
Under a Roman census decree, Joseph and the pregnant Mary travel to Bethlehem, David's city; Jesus is
born and laid in a feeding trough "because there was no room for them in the inn." An angel announces
the birth to shepherds keeping watch nearby, followed by "a multitude of the heavenly army" praising
God; the shepherds go, find the child, and spread the news.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 2:6-7 (WEB): "While they were there, the day had come for her to give birth. She gave birth to
  her firstborn son. She wrapped him in bands of cloth and laid him in a feeding trough, because
  there was no room for them in the inn."
- Luke 2:10-11 (WEB): "The angel said to them, 'Don't be afraid, for behold, I bring you good news of
  great joy which will be to all the people. For there is born to you today, in David's city, a
  Savior, who is Christ the Lord.'"
- Luke 2:13-14 (WEB): "Suddenly, there was with the angel a multitude of the heavenly army praising
  God and saying, 'Glory to God in the highest, on earth peace, good will toward men.'"
- Luke 2:19-20 (WEB): "But Mary kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart. The shepherds
  returned, glorifying and praising God for all the things that they had heard and seen, just as it
  was told them."

### 5. Simeon and Anna at the Temple (Luke 2:22-38)
Mary and Joseph bring the infant Jesus to the Jerusalem temple per the law of Moses. Simeon, a
righteous man told by the Holy Spirit he would not die before seeing "the Lord's Christ," takes the
child in his arms and blesses God; he also tells Mary that a "sword will pierce through your own
soul." The elderly prophetess Anna, who worshiped in the temple continually, also recognizes the
child and speaks of him to others awaiting Jerusalem's redemption.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 2:29-32 (WEB): "Now you are releasing your servant, Master, according to your word, in peace;
  for my eyes have seen your salvation, which you have prepared before the face of all peoples; a
  light for revelation to the nations, and the glory of your people Israel."
- Luke 2:34-35 (WEB): "Simeon blessed them, and said to Mary, his mother, 'Behold, this child is
  appointed for the falling and the rising of many in Israel, and for a sign which is spoken against.
  Yes, a sword will pierce through your own soul, that the thoughts of many hearts may be
  revealed.'"

### 6. The Boy Jesus in the Temple (Luke 2:41-52)
At age twelve, Jesus stays behind in Jerusalem after the Passover feast without his parents'
knowledge; they find him three days later in the temple, "sitting in the middle of the teachers,
both listening to them and asking them questions." Jesus tells his anxious parents he "must be in
my Father's house." He returns with them to Nazareth and grows in wisdom and favor.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 2:46-47 (WEB): "After three days they found him in the temple, sitting in the middle of the
  teachers, both listening to them and asking them questions. All who heard him were amazed at his
  understanding and his answers."
- Luke 2:48-49 (WEB): "When they saw him, they were astonished; and his mother said to him, 'Son,
  why have you treated us this way? Behold, your father and I were anxiously looking for you.' He

  house?'"
- Luke 2:51-52 (WEB): "And he went down with them and came to Nazareth. He was subject to them, and
  his mother kept all these sayings in her heart. And Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in
  favor with God and men."

### 8. The Temptation in the Wilderness (Luke 4:1-13)
Full of the Holy Spirit, Jesus is led into the wilderness and fasts forty days; the devil tempts him
three times (to turn stone to bread, to gain worldly authority by worshiping the devil, to test God
by leaping from the temple). Each time, Jesus answers by quoting Deuteronomy. The devil departs
"until another time."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 4:3-4 (WEB): "The devil said to him, 'If you are the Son of God, command this stone to become
  bread.' Jesus answered him, saying, 'It is written, "Man shall not live by bread alone, but by
  every word of God."'"
- Luke 4:8 (WEB): "Jesus answered him, 'Get behind me, Satan! For it is written, "You shall worship
  the Lord your God, and you shall serve him only."'"
- Luke 4:12-13 (WEB): "Jesus answering, said to him, 'It has been said, "You shall not tempt the Lord
  your God."' When the devil had completed every temptation, he departed from him until another
  time."

### 9. Jesus's Inaugural Sermon at Nazareth (Luke 4:16-30)
In his hometown synagogue, Jesus reads from Isaiah ("The Spirit of the Lord is on me... to preach
good news to the poor") and declares, "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing."
The crowd's initial admiration turns to fury when Jesus cites Elijah and Elisha helping non-Israelite
outsiders (a widow in Sidon, Naaman the Syrian); they try to throw him off a cliff, but he walks
through the crowd unharmed. **This scene is Luke's own explicit statement of the poor/outsider theme
this brief highlights throughout — see Contested Territory: The Nazareth Sermon's Self-Application
below for how the "fulfilled" claim is bounded.**

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 4:18-19 (WEB): "The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good
  news to the poor. He has sent me to heal the broken hearted, to proclaim release to the captives,
  recovering of sight to the blind, to deliver those who are crushed, and to proclaim the acceptable
  year of the Lord."
- Luke 4:21 (WEB): "He began to tell them, 'Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your
  hearing.'" *(Handled per the format restriction below — see Contested Territory: The Nazareth
  Sermon's Self-Application.)*
- Luke 4:24 (WEB): "He said, 'Most certainly I tell you, no prophet is acceptable in his hometown.'"

### 10. The Calling of the First Disciples (Luke 5:1-11)
Jesus teaches from Simon Peter's boat, then tells him to "put out into the deep" for a catch. Despite
a fruitless night of fishing, Peter obeys; the catch is so large the nets begin to break. Peter falls
at Jesus's knees in astonishment ("Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord"); Jesus tells him he
will now "catch people alive." Peter, James, and John leave everything to follow him.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 5:4-5 (WEB): "When he had finished speaking, he said to Simon, 'Put out into the deep and let

- Luke 5:8 (WEB): "But Simon Peter, when he saw it, fell down at Jesus' knees, saying, 'Depart from

- Luke 5:10-11 (WEB): "Jesus said to Simon, 'Don't be afraid. From now on you will be catching people

### 11. The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25-37)
A lawyer, testing Jesus, asks what is required to "inherit eternal life"; Jesus points him to loving
God and neighbor, and the lawyer, "desiring to justify himself," asks "who is my neighbor?" Jesus
answers with the parable: a man beaten and left for dead is passed by a priest and a Levite, but
helped, at cost and personal risk, by a Samaritan — a group with whom Jews of the era had deep
religious and ethnic hostility, per the text's implied contrast. Jesus tells the lawyer to "go and do
likewise."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Luke 10:27 (WEB): "He answered, 'You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all
  your soul, with all your strength, and with all your mind; and your neighbor as yourself.'"
- Luke 10:33-34 (WEB): "But a certain Samaritan, as he traveled, came where he was. When he saw him,
  he was moved with compassion, came to him, and bound up his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. He
  set him on his own animal, brought him to an inn, and took care of him."
- Luke 10:36-37 (WEB): "'Now which of these three do you think seemed to be a neighbor to him who
  fell among the robbers?' He said, 'He who showed mercy on him.' Then Jesus said to him, 'Go and do
  likewise.'"

### 12. Mary and Martha (Luke 10:38-42)

**Challenge-ready verses:**

### 13. The Lost Sheep, the Lost Coin, and the Prodigal Son (Luke 15:1-32)
Responding to Pharisees' complaints that he "welcomes sinners, and eats with them," Jesus tells three

obedient son objects to the celebration.

**Challenge-ready verses:**

- Luke 15:18-19 (WEB): "'I will get up and go to my father, and will tell him, "Father, I have

### 14. The Rich Man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19-31)

### 18. The Empty Tomb and the Road to Emmaus (Luke 24:1-35)

bread," at which point he vanishes. They return to Jerusalem to report it. **Luke's resurrection
account is distinctively built around this Emmaus scene, found in no other Gospel.**

Boss battle (world 42 capstone): a mixed-format sequence spanning all 18 pivotal scenes, weighted
toward level 3-5 items. Given Luke's unusually high proportion of format-restricted verses (nine of
the eighteen scenes carry at least one recall-only item, per the Contested Territory bounding
below — the highest proportion of any book this project has produced, reflecting the NT's higher
doctrinal-review bar per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7), expect a materially higher share of
recall-format items relative to open multiple-choice than in any OT book this project has shipped,
similar in kind to (but larger in scale than) the Hosea precedent.

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 and §6 (New Testament Addendum) — flagged for the theological
reviewer, not resolved here. Every category named in the style guide's §6 list is addressed below,
either as a live item this brief bounds or as an explicit "not primarily present in this book" note.

- **The Virgin Birth (Luke 1:26-38).** Per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7, this is the central case
  where the OT's usual escape hatch — excluding a *later* Christian reading layered onto the text —
  doesn't apply, because the virgin conception **is the primary text's own claim**, not a later
  interpretation of it. This brief's bounding, per this assignment's explicit instruction: narrate
  what the text itself states (Mary is a virgin; Gabriel states the child will be conceived "by the
  Holy Spirit," not by Joseph; Mary consents) as the text's own narrated claim, the same way this
  project already reports the resurrection, the flood, or any other narrated event — **no challenge
  item asks the player to affirm the virgin birth as a resolved historical-critical fact, and none
  asks "how" or "why" beyond the text's own stated mechanism.** **Structural rule: Luke 1:32-33 and
  1:34-35 are restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only — never a multiple-choice "what does
  this mean" or "was this historically/biologically possible" format** — the same treatment this
  project has given every messianic-identity or Trinity-adjacent verse (Genesis 1:27, Genesis 3:15,
  Hosea 3:5, Hosea 11:1, Ezekiel 34:23-24). Narrative-fact items about the *surrounding* scene (who
  appeared, to whom, in what town, what name was given) remain open recall/multiple-choice, since
  those carry no doctrinal freight on their own.

- **The Baptism and the Voice from Heaven (Luke 3:21-22).** "You are my beloved Son" is a Trinity-
  adjacent divine-sonship declaration structurally similar to 1:32-35 above. **Structural rule: Luke
  3:21-22 is restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only** — consistent with the same prudence
  rule Genesis applied to 1:27 (a verse "often invoked in present-day debates unrelated to the
  book's content," per `docs/content/genesis.md`) and the virgin-birth rule immediately above.

- **The Nazareth Sermon's Self-Application (Luke 4:16-30, esp. 4:21).** Jesus's claim "today, this
  Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" is the text's own narrated claim about Jesus's
  identity and mission — again, primary text, not a later reading (contrast this with how Genesis
  22:14 records Abraham's own words, or how Hosea 11:1 was excluded from NT-fulfillment framing
  precisely because *Matthew*, not Hosea, makes that connection). Because Luke's own text makes the
  claim, narrating it is in-bounds; grading whether the claim is *true* is not. **Structural rule:
  Luke 4:21 is restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only** — the challenge can test that Jesus
  said this, and quote what he said, never a multiple-choice item asking what the claim "means" or
  whether it was fulfilled.

- **Atonement Theory and the Last Supper's Institution Words (Luke 22:19-20).** "This is my body
  which is given for you... this cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you"
  is sacrificial/covenantal language that different Christian traditions read through different
  atonement frameworks (penal substitution, Christus Victor, ransom, moral-influence — per
  `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's atonement-theory category) without the text itself adjudicating
  between them. This scene is also the origin point of the Eucharist/Lord's Supper, where Catholic
  and Orthodox tradition read "this is my body" as pointing toward real presence and most
  (not all) Protestant traditions read it as memorial/symbolic — a live sacramental-theology
  divide named explicitly in the style guide's NT addendum. **Structural rule: Luke 22:19-20 is
  restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only — never a multiple-choice "what do these words
  mean/accomplish" or "what happens to the bread and wine" format.** Narrative-fact items about the
  surrounding scene (that it was a Passover meal, that Jesus said he would not eat it again until
  God's Kingdom comes, that he foretold his betrayal) remain open format.

- **"Today You Will Be with Me in Paradise" (Luke 23:42-43).** The repentant thief's immediate
  entry into "Paradise" upon death touches two live cross-tradition questions at once: (1) the
  faith-vs-works soteriology fault line named in the style guide's addendum — the thief is saved
  with no opportunity for any work, baptism, or church membership, a passage Protestant tradition
  has long read as a strong faith-alone case, while Catholic and Orthodox tradition harmonize it
  without abandoning sacramental or synergistic frameworks; and (2) exactly what "Paradise" refers
  to and when it is entered — immediate presence with Christ vs. an intermediate state — differs
  across traditions (including, historically, where Luke 16:19-31's "Abraham's bosom" fits relative
  to it, a question this brief does not attempt to resolve by placing the two scenes in relation to
  each other). **Structural rule: Luke 23:42-43 is restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only —
  never a multiple-choice item about what "Paradise" means, when it is entered, or what the episode
  proves about how salvation works.**

- **The Resurrection Announcement and the Empty Tomb (Luke 24:1-12).** Per the same `CANON_STRUCTURE`
  §7 logic as the virgin birth: the resurrection **is the primary text's own claim**, not a later
  reading. This brief narrates what the text states happened (the tomb was found empty; two men in
  dazzling clothing announced "he isn't here, but is risen"; the women reported it; the apostles
  initially disbelieved; Peter went and saw the empty wrappings) without any challenge item asking
  the player to affirm the resurrection as a resolved historical-critical fact beyond what the text
  narrates. **Structural rule: Luke 24:5-6 (the angelic announcement itself) is restricted to
  recall/fill-in-blank format only.** Surrounding narrative-fact items (who found the tomb, what
  Peter did, who doubted) remain open format, since those are uncontested narrative detail rather
  than the truth-claim itself.

- **Ecclesiology and Gender: Women as First Witnesses (Luke 24:1-11, also 8:1-3, not separately
  scened above).** Luke names women (Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James) as the first
  witnesses to the empty tomb, and states plainly that the male apostles dismissed their report as
  "nonsense" (24:11) before Peter went to check himself. This is directly adjacent to the
  ecclesiology/gender-role category the style guide's addendum names (though the addendum's primary
  examples — 1 Timothy 2/3, Titus 1, 1 Corinthians 11/14 — are Pauline church-office passages not
  present in Luke at all). **This brief's bounding:** the challenge items test only the narrative
  fact (who found the tomb, what they reported, that the apostles initially didn't believe them) —
  no challenge item asks whether this scene establishes, implies, or undermines any position on
  women's teaching or ecclesial authority. Kept in open format (not recall-restricted), since the
  narrative fact itself is uncontested across traditions — the *inference* some readers draw from it
  is what's contested, and this brief draws no inference.

- **Sacraments/Ordinances Beyond the Last Supper: Baptism.** John's baptism (Luke 3:1-22) touches the
  mode/timing/regeneration-vs-symbol questions the style guide's addendum names, but Luke's own text
  presents it plainly as "a baptism of repentance for remission of sins" preceding and distinct from
  specifically Christian baptism (which Luke's second volume, Acts, addresses more directly — out of
  scope for this book). This brief's items (3:7-8, 3:16, 3:21-22) test only the narrated content —
  John's preaching, his stated relationship to "one mightier," and the baptism-of-Jesus event itself
  — never a claim about what baptism accomplishes theologically. No additional format restriction
  needed beyond the Trinity-adjacent rule already applied to 3:21-22 above.

- **Spiritual Gifts.** Not primarily present in this book. Luke narrates the Spirit's empowerment of
  Jesus himself (4:1, 4:18) and, at the book's close, Jesus's promise that the disciples will be
  "clothed with power from on high" (24:49) — but this promise is explicitly forward-pointing,
  fulfilled in Acts 2 (Pentecost), the next book in this project's NT sequence per
  `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7. The cessationism/continuationism live divide the style guide's
  addendum names centers on 1 Corinthians 12-14's content (tongues, prophecy, healing gifts
  distributed among believers), which has no real parallel in Luke's own text. No challenge item in
  this brief touches this category; deferred to the Acts and 1 Corinthians briefs.

- **Predestination/Free Will.** Not primarily present in this book in the way Romans 9 raises it (the
  style guide's addendum's named example). Luke's Gethsemane scene (22:41-42, "not my will, but
  yours, be done") touches divine will vs. human will in a narrow, Christologically-specific way
  (Jesus's own will in his own passion, not a general doctrine of human predestination), which this
  brief's item narrates as the text's own words without extending it into a general free-will/
  predestination claim. No additional format restriction needed beyond ordinary narrative-fact
  bounding.

- **Eschatology.** Luke 21 (the Olivet discourse — signs of the end, "days of vengeance," "times of
  the Gentiles") is genuinely present in the book but **deliberately excluded from this brief's
  pivotal scenes and challenge-ready verses entirely**, rather than narrowly bounded, consistent
  with `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's guidance that eschatological material (pre-/mid-/post-
  tribulation timing, millennium views) is "the single most fragmented interpretive territory in all
  of Christian theology" and its specific warning to expect the most conservative treatment for this
  category across the NT expansion. This is the same "exclude rather than narrowly bound" posture
  the guide reserves specifically for Revelation-adjacent material — applied here pre-emptively
  rather than waiting for a reviewer finding, since Luke 21's content is structurally identical to
  the parallel passages in Matthew 24/Mark 13 that the Matthew and Mark briefs (authored in parallel
  by separate agents) will need to make the same call on independently.

- **Three Manuscript-Disputed Passages Luke Adds to the List (independent finding, beyond the three
  named cases; confirmed during theological review, see `docs/reviews/luke-review.md` §3.9).** The
  style guide's addendum names three known textual-criticism cases (Mark 16:9-20, John 7:53-8:11,
  1 John 5:7-8) — none of which occur in Luke. This brief independently flags three passages within
  Luke's own text with a comparable, genuine manuscript-tradition question, each confirmed present
  in WEB without any bracket or footnote (checked directly against ebible.org and biblegateway.com,
  see the review file §1 for the fetch record) and each independently confirmed via dedicated
  textual-criticism sourcing (review file §3.9), not asserted from this brief's own authority:
  - **Luke 22:19b-20** (the longer institution-of-the-Eucharist text, "which is given for
    you... do this in memory of me" through the cup saying) is omitted in some Western manuscripts
    (a "Western non-interpolation," per the Catholic USCCB's own study note on this passage, cited
    in the review file), while the majority of manuscripts — including the earliest surviving papyrus
    of Luke — carry the longer reading WEB prints. This sits directly alongside, and is distinct
    from, the sacramental-theology bounding already applied to this scene above.
  - **Luke 22:43-44** (the angel strengthening Jesus in Gethsemane, and his sweat becoming "like
    great drops of blood") is absent from a number of the earliest and most highly regarded Greek
    manuscripts, per multiple independent textual-criticism sources (review file §3.9). Not
    selected as a challenge-ready verse in this brief (22:41-42 already covers this scene's
    narrative content), so no format restriction is needed — noted here for transparency only.
  - **Luke 23:34's first sentence** ("Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are
    doing") is likewise absent from some of the earliest manuscripts (including, per standard
    textual-critical scholarship, Codex Vaticanus and P75), though present in the majority of later
    manuscripts and in WEB without a bracket.
  All three are retained in this brief where used, consistent with the principle that a manuscript
  question is a text-integrity issue to disclose, not automatically an exclusion trigger (the style
  guide's own treatment of Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 is to check what WEB prints and disclose
  the issue, not to exclude by default) — and each is already covered by the recall-only or
  narrative-fact-only bounding applied above for independent doctrinal reasons.

- **Historicity of the Census (Luke 2:1-2).** Luke dates Jesus's birth to a census "when Quirinius
  was governor of Syria" under Caesar Augustus — a detail with a well-known historical/chronological
  question among scholars (the dating of Quirinius's governorship relative to Herod the Great's
  death) that is a historicity/scholarship question, not a doctrinal or denominational one in the
  sense the style guide is built to guard against (the same category as the OT's Ezekiel 4:4-6
  day-count precedent or Hosea's composition-history note). This brief's item (2:6-7, used above)
  tests only the narrated birth event itself, not the census's historical dating. No format
  restriction needed.
