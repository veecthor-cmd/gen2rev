## Setting / Era

Proverbs is wisdom literature attributed principally to Solomon (with later collections added,
per the book's own internal headings — e.g. 22:17, 25:1, 30:1, 31:1), traditionally dated to
Israel's united-monarchy and post-monarchy period. Like Leviticus, **Proverbs is not narrative** —
it has almost no plot or sequence of events. Per this book's established precedent
(`docs/reviews/leviticus-review.md`), this brief structures the "Pivotal Scenes" as **pivotal
thematic clusters**: groupings of well-known, uncontested proverbs around a shared theme, rather
than a sequence of narrated events. There is no "order things happen in" for a sequencing
challenge type the way Genesis or Joshua has one — see the Difficulty Ladder below for how
sequencing is adapted for a proverb collection instead.

## Pivotal Thematic Clusters (5)

### 1. The Beginning and Value of Wisdom (Proverbs 1, 3)
The book's programmatic opening statement, followed by its most quoted call to trust and its
description of wisdom as more valuable than any wealth.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Proverbs 1:7 (WEB): "The fear of Yahweh is the beginning of knowledge, but the foolish despise
  wisdom and instruction."
- Proverbs 3:5-6 (WEB): "Trust in Yahweh with all your heart, and don't lean on your own
  understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight."
- Proverbs 3:13-15 (WEB): "Happy is the man who finds wisdom, the man who gets understanding. For
  her good profit is better than getting silver, and her return is better than fine gold. She is
  more precious than rubies. None of the things you can desire are to be compared to her."

### 2. Guard Your Heart, Walk the Right Path (Proverbs 4)
A father's instruction on wisdom's protective role, using the image of two contrasting paths.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Proverbs 4:18-19 (WEB): "But the path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines
  more and more until the perfect day. The way of the wicked is like darkness. They don't know
  what they stumble over."
- Proverbs 4:23 (WEB): "Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of
  life."

### 3. Well-Known Wisdom Sayings (Proverbs 15-17, 27)
A cluster of Proverbs' most widely recognized standalone sayings on speech, humility, friendship,
and character — representative of the book's core literary form, the two-line proverb.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Proverbs 15:1 (WEB): "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger."
- Proverbs 16:18 (WEB): "Pride goes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall."
- Proverbs 17:17 (WEB): "A friend loves at all times; and a brother is born for adversity."
- Proverbs 27:17 (WEB): "Iron sharpens iron; so a man sharpens his friend's countenance."

### 4. Instruction for the Young: "Train Up a Child" (Proverbs 22)
From the "Sayings of the Wise" section (22:17 onward introduces a new collection), including the
book's best-known verse about raising children and one on the value of a good name.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Proverbs 22:1 (WEB): "A good name is more desirable than great riches, and loving favor is
  better than silver and gold."
- Proverbs 22:6 (WEB): "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not
  depart from it."

### 5. The Virtuous Woman (Proverbs 31)
The book's closing poem (an acrostic in the original Hebrew), praising a capable, wise woman —
Proverbs' culminating picture of wisdom lived out in daily life.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Proverbs 31:10 (WEB): "Who can find a worthy woman? For her value is far above rubies."
- Proverbs 31:25-26 (WEB): "Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come.
  She opens her mouth with wisdom. Kind instruction is on her tongue."
- Proverbs 31:30 (WEB): "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman who fears Yahweh, she
  shall be praised."

## Difficulty Ladder

Because Proverbs has no narrative sequence, "sequence" items here order *clusters as they appear
in the book* or *lines within a single proverb's parallelism*, not plot events — flagged as the
same kind of structural adaptation Leviticus needed, not an oversight.

| Level | Type | Example (from this book) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 — Recall | Verse fill-in-blank | "Trust in ___ with all your heart." (Prov 3:5) → Yahweh |
| 2 — Recall | Multiple choice, attribution | "Which verse says 'a friend loves at all times'?" → Proverbs 17:17 |
| 3 — Sequence | Order the two halves of a single proverb's parallelism | Given "Pride goes before destruction" and "an arrogant spirit before a fall," place them in the order Prov 16:18 states them |
| 4 — Sequence + recall | Order + attribute a quotation | Given three quotations (1:7, 3:5-6, 22:6), place them in the order they occur in the book and match each to its reference |
| 5 — Recall on textual detail | Multiple choice | "According to Proverbs 31:30, what does the text say is deceitful?" → charm (tests attentive reading of the text, not an application judgment — see Contested Territory) |

Boss battle (world 20 capstone): a mixed-format round spanning all 5 clusters, weighted toward
level 3-5 items, drawing at least one verse from each cluster so all five are represented.

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **Proverbs as promise vs. general wisdom.** This is the single biggest risk specific to this
  book, per this assignment's brief. Proverbs is wisdom literature — general observations about
  how life usually works, not unconditional divine guarantees (e.g. Proverbs 22:6, "train up a
  child," is widely read across traditions as a general principle, not a promise that a
  specific child's outcome is guaranteed regardless of choices; the book's own genre — collected
  "sayings of the wise," per 1:6 — supports this). Some traditions and popular usage treat
  individual proverbs more like promises than others do. **No challenge item in this brief is
  built in "what should you do" / application-style framing that would imply a proverb is a
  guaranteed outcome rather than a general wisdom saying.** Every item stays at direct-recall or
  attribution level (what the text says, word for word, and where) — never "what does this verse
  promise you" or "what will happen if you do X." **Structural rule (added per theological
  self-review, `docs/reviews/proverbs-review.md`): Proverbs 22:6 may only be used in
  recall/fill-in-blank or attribution-matching format (as in Difficulty Ladder level 4) — never
  presented as a multiple-choice "what will happen if a parent does this" or any other
  application-framed format.**
- **The Solomon/multiple-author question (1:1, 22:17, 25:1, 30:1, 31:1).** Mainstream and critical
  scholarship diverge on how much of the book is directly Solomonic versus a later compiled
  collection under his name/patronage. This brief does not build any challenge item that asserts a
  single-author claim beyond what the text's own internal headings state (e.g. 1:1 attributes the
  primary collection to Solomon; later headings name other sources) — items test only what the
  text's heading says, not a scholarly authorship conclusion.
- **The virtuous woman poem (Proverbs 31) as a gender-role prescription.** This poem is sometimes
  read in present-day discourse as prescribing a specific domestic role for women; other readings
  treat it as a poetic ideal of wisdom-in-action using the domestic-management imagery of its own
  ancient context, not a universal prescription. No challenge item asks the player to evaluate or
  affirm what the poem prescribes about women's roles — items are direct-recall on the poem's own
  text (what it says a wise woman does, per the text), never framed as "what should a woman do."
  **Structural rule (added per theological self-review): every challenge item drawn from Proverbs
  31:10-31 must be phrased as recall of the poem's own wording ("according to the text, what does
  she do/say") — never as an evaluative or prescriptive question ("what should a woman do," "is
  this a good role model").**
- **"Fear of Yahweh" (1:7, 31:30).** The Hebrew concept ("yir'ah") is read across traditions as
  reverent awe rather than terror, but this is itself an interpretive gloss beyond the bare text.
  No challenge item asks the player to define or characterize what "fear of Yahweh" means — items
  test only that the phrase appears and where, not its theological content.
