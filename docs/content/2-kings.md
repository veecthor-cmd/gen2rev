## Sensitivity note (read before the scenes below)

This book's second half narrates two national military catastrophes — the Assyrian conquest of the
northern kingdom and the Babylonian conquest of Jerusalem — including a siege, famine, mass
deportation, and (in 2 Kings 19) a mass death of an attacking army attributed to Yahweh's angel.
Per the assignment's instruction, this brief keeps all challenge-ready verses factual (what
happened, to whom, in what order) rather than graphic. Specifically excluded from challenge-ready
content: 2 Kings 25:6-7 (Zedekiah's sons killed before him, then his own eyes put out — graphic
violence, not selected for any challenge item or scene description below) and 2 Kings 6:26-29
(cannibalism during the earlier siege of Samaria under Ben-Hadad — not part of any scene in this
brief at all). Also explicitly excluded: 2 Kings 2:23-24 (children mocking Elisha, then mauled by
bears) — not selected as a scene or challenge item anywhere in this brief, flagged here so it is not
silently added later without review, given this is a child-directed game.

## Setting / Era

2 Kings continues directly from 1 Kings (World 11), opening with Elijah's final act as prophet —
passing his role to Elisha — then following the two kingdoms established at the split through their
remaining history. The northern kingdom (Israel) never has a righteous king by the text's own
repeated assessment, and falls to the Assyrian Empire partway through the book. The southern
kingdom (Judah) continues longer, with two notable reforming kings, Hezekiah and Josiah, before it
too falls, to Babylon, at the book's end. As World 12, this book is a natural capstone for the
entire 14-world MVP arc: it closes out the "kingdom era" narrative that began with Israel's judges
and kings (Worlds 8–11) and, in its final scene, ends the story on the exile — the point at which
the promised land, the monarchy, and the temple are all, for now, lost.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. Elijah's Ascension and Elisha's Succession (2 Kings 2:1-15)
As Elijah's ministry ends, he offers Elisha, his attendant and successor, one last request. Elisha
asks for a double portion of Elijah's spirit. A chariot and horses of fire appear and Elijah is
taken up in a whirlwind; Elisha picks up Elijah's fallen mantle, strikes the Jordan with it as
Elijah had done, and the other prophets recognize that Elijah's spirit now rests on him.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Kings 2:9 (WEB, trimmed): "'Ask what I shall do for you, before I am taken from you.' Elisha
  said, 'Please let a double portion of your spirit be on me.'"
- 2 Kings 2:11 (WEB): "As they continued on and talked, behold, a chariot of fire and horses of fire
  separated them; and Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven."
- 2 Kings 2:15 (WEB, trimmed): "When the sons of the prophets who were at Jericho facing him saw
  him, they said, 'The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha.'"

### 2. The Fall of the Northern Kingdom, Israel (2 Kings 17:5-23)
After a three-year siege, the king of Assyria captures Samaria, Israel's capital, and deports the
population. The text states plainly that this happened because Israel had persisted in worshiping
other gods despite repeated prophetic warning.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Kings 17:5-6 (WEB, trimmed): "The king of Assyria came up throughout all the land, went up to
  Samaria, and besieged it three years. In the ninth year of Hoshea the king of Assyria took Samaria
  and carried Israel away to Assyria."
- 2 Kings 17:7 (WEB, trimmed): "It was so because the children of Israel had sinned against Yahweh
  their God... and had feared other gods."
- 2 Kings 17:23 (WEB, trimmed): "Yahweh removed Israel out of his sight, as he said by all his
  servants the prophets. So Israel was carried away out of their own land to Assyria to this day."

### 3. Hezekiah and the Assyrian Siege of Jerusalem (2 Kings 18-19)
Assyria, having taken the northern kingdom, later invades Judah and threatens Jerusalem itself under
King Hezekiah. Hezekiah takes the Assyrian king's threatening letter into the temple and prays for
deliverance. That night, the text says, Yahweh's angel strikes down the Assyrian camp, and the siege
ends without the city falling. (This brief emphasizes the siege/deliverance narrative; 1–2
Chronicles' brief is expected to cover Hezekiah's temple-cleansing and Passover reforms instead —
see Differentiation note above.)

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Kings 19:15 (WEB, trimmed): "Yahweh, the God of Israel, who are enthroned above the cherubim,
  you are the God, even you alone, of all the kingdoms of the earth."
- 2 Kings 19:19 (WEB): "Now therefore, Yahweh our God, save us, I beg you, out of his hand, that all
  the kingdoms of the earth may know that you, Yahweh, are God alone."
- 2 Kings 19:35-36 (WEB, trimmed): "That night, Yahweh's angel went out and struck one hundred
  eighty-five thousand in the camp of the Assyrians... So Sennacherib king of Assyria departed, went
  home, and lived at Nineveh."

### 4. Josiah's Reforms and the Rediscovery of the Law (2 Kings 22-23)
During temple repairs under King Josiah, the high priest Hilkiah finds a scroll of the Law. When it
is read to Josiah, he tears his clothes in distress at how far the nation has strayed from it, then
leads the people in publicly renewing the covenant and removing idolatrous worship sites across the
land. (This brief emphasizes the Law's rediscovery and the covenant renewal; 1–2 Chronicles' brief
is expected to give fuller treatment to the Passover Josiah then celebrates — see Differentiation
note above.)

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Kings 22:8 (WEB): "Hilkiah the high priest said to Shaphan the scribe, 'I have found the book of
  the law in Yahweh's house.' Hilkiah delivered the book to Shaphan, and he read it."
- 2 Kings 22:11 (WEB): "When the king had heard the words of the book of the law, he tore his
  clothes."
- 2 Kings 23:3 (WEB, trimmed): "The king stood by the pillar and made a covenant before Yahweh to
  walk after Yahweh and to keep his commandments, his testimonies, and his statutes with all his
  heart and all his soul... and all the people agreed to the covenant."

### 5. The Fall of Jerusalem and the Babylonian Exile (2 Kings 25) — capstone scene
Decades after Josiah, Babylon besieges and finally breaches Jerusalem. The temple Solomon built, the
king's house, and the city's great houses are burned; its walls are broken down. The book's closing
verses report that years later, in Babylon, the exiled Judean king Jehoiachin is released from
prison and treated kindly by the Babylonian king for the rest of his life — an ending on a note of
survival and mild grace, but not restoration. As this brief's final scene and the 14-world MVP's own
capstone, this is intended to land as the story's most serious beat: the land, monarchy, and temple
that the previous eleven worlds built toward are, for now, all lost.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- 2 Kings 25:8-9 (WEB, trimmed): "Nebuzaradan the captain of the guard, a servant of the king of
  Babylon, came to Jerusalem. He burned Yahweh's house, the king's house, and all the houses of
  Jerusalem."
- 2 Kings 25:10 (WEB): "All the army of the Chaldeans, who were with the captain of the guard, broke
  down the walls around Jerusalem."
- 2 Kings 25:27, 25:29 (WEB, trimmed): "In the thirty-seventh year of the captivity of Jehoiachin
  king of Judah... Evilmerodach king of Babylon... released Jehoiachin king of Judah out of
  prison... and changed his prison garments. Jehoiachin ate bread before him continually all the
  days of his life."

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **2 Kings 17:7-23, the fall of Israel's stated cause.** The text's narrator directly states the
  fall happened because of persistent idolatry — this is the text's own summary judgment, not an
  outside interpretation layered on top, so it is in-bounds as narrative fact per the style guide.
  Still flagging because it sits close to the line between "narrative fact" and "theological
  causation claim." This brief's challenge items (17:5-6, 17:7, 17:23) test only what the text
  states happened and why the text itself says so, never an extension to whether national
  misfortune generally indicates divine judgment.
- **2 Kings 19:35, the death of 185,000 Assyrian soldiers.** A mass-casualty event attributed to
  Yahweh's angel. Per the sensitivity note above, the challenge verse is trimmed to the bare fact
  reported (the number, and that Sennacherib withdrew) with no elaboration on how the deaths
  occurred.
- **2 Kings 25:27-30 vs. 2 Chronicles 36:22-23 — two different endings to the exile narrative.**
  Checked directly against both books' text (see review): 2 Kings ends on Jehoiachin's release and
  kind treatment in Babylon — survival, not restoration. 2 Chronicles ends with Cyrus's decree
  permitting the exiles to return and rebuild the temple — a comparatively hope-forward ending. This
  is a genuine, confirmed textual difference between the two books' closing notes, not an
  interpretive claim; flagging it here so the difference is deliberate framing for both this book's
  brief and 2 Chronicles' brief, not a coincidence of scene selection.
- **2 Kings 2, Elijah/Elisha succession — no New Testament typology.** Some Christian traditions
  connect Elijah's ascension and Elisha's succession typologically to later figures (e.g. John the
  Baptist, or Gospel transfiguration scenes). Since the MVP is OT-only, this brief's scene and
  challenge items describe only what 2 Kings 2 itself narrates, with no forward-looking typological
  framing.
- **Exclusions stated explicitly (see Sensitivity note above):** 2 Kings 25:6-7 (Zedekiah's sons
  killed, his eyes put out), 2 Kings 6:26-29 (cannibalism during an earlier siege), and 2 Kings
  2:23-24 (children mauled by bears) are not part of any scene or challenge item in this brief.
