## Setting / Era

Jonah is set during the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (8th century BC — Jonah the son of Amittai
is named in 2 Kings 14:25 as a prophet from that period), and centers on Nineveh, the great city of
the Assyrian empire, Israel's most feared foreign power. Unlike the other eleven Minor Prophets,
which are collections of oracles, Jonah is a complete, self-contained third-person narrative — one
prophet, one mission, one story arc — closer in shape to Ruth or Esther than to Hosea or Amos. Per
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md`'s Minor Prophets row, this is exactly why Jonah gets its own dedicated
authoring pass rather than the thinner treatment expected for most of Wave 3's other eleven books.

As World 32, Jonah sits within the Minor Prophets group of the Old Testament expansion (Wave 3:
Hosea through Malachi, 12 books, worlds 28-39). This brief tells Jonah's story entirely on its own
terms, within its own book: God's mercy extends even to Israel's enemies when they repent, and
Jonah's anger at that mercy becomes the book's own final subject. No New Testament material is
referenced or implied anywhere in this brief, consistent with the product's OT-only scope.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. The Call and the Flight (Jonah 1:1-3)
Yahweh commands Jonah to go to Nineveh and preach against its wickedness. Jonah instead flees in
the opposite direction, boarding a ship at Joppa bound for Tarshish, away from Yahweh's presence.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Jonah 1:1-2 (WEB): "Now Yahweh's word came to Jonah the son of Amittai, saying, 'Arise, go to
  Nineveh, that great city, and preach against it, for their wickedness has come up before me.'"
- Jonah 1:3 (WEB): "But Jonah rose up to flee to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh. He went down
  to Joppa, and found a ship going to Tarshish; so he paid its fare, and went down into it, to go
  with them to Tarshish from the presence of Yahweh."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Jonah 1](https://ebible.org/web/JON01.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Jonah 1:1-3](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+1&version=WEB)
— exact match, two independent sources.*

### 2. The Storm and the Sailors (Jonah 1:4-16)
A great storm threatens to break up the ship. The pagan sailors pray each to his own god and cast
lots to find the cause; the lot falls on Jonah, who admits he is fleeing Yahweh and tells them to
throw him into the sea. They do, reluctantly, and the storm ceases; the sailors then fear Yahweh,
offer a sacrifice, and make vows to him.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Jonah 1:9 (WEB): "He said to them, 'I am a Hebrew, and I fear Yahweh, the God of heaven, who has
  made the sea and the dry land.'"
- Jonah 1:12 (WEB): "He said to them, 'Take me up, and throw me into the sea. Then the sea will be
  calm for you; for I know that because of me this great storm is on you.'"
- Jonah 1:15-16 (WEB): "So they took up Jonah and threw him into the sea; and the sea ceased its
  raging. Then the men feared Yahweh exceedingly; and they offered a sacrifice to Yahweh and made
  vows."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Jonah 1](https://ebible.org/web/JON01.htm).*

### 3. The Fish and Jonah's Prayer (Jonah 1:17-2:10)
Yahweh appoints a huge fish to swallow Jonah, who is inside it three days and three nights. From
inside the fish, Jonah prays a psalm-like prayer of distress and thanksgiving, recalling his
descent toward death and Yahweh's rescue. Yahweh then speaks to the fish, which vomits Jonah out
onto dry land.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Jonah 1:17 (WEB): "Yahweh prepared a huge fish to swallow up Jonah, and Jonah was in the belly of
  the fish three days and three nights."
- Jonah 2:2 (WEB): "He said, 'I called because of my affliction to Yahweh. He answered me. Out of
  the belly of Sheol I cried. You heard my voice.'"
- Jonah 2:9 (WEB): "But I will sacrifice to you with the voice of thanksgiving. I will pay that
  which I have vowed. Salvation belongs to Yahweh."
- Jonah 2:10 (WEB): "Then Yahweh spoke to the fish, and it vomited out Jonah on the dry land."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Jonah 2](https://ebible.org/web/JON02.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Jonah 2:1-2, 2:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+2&version=WEB)
— exact match, two independent sources.*

### 4. The Call Renewed and Nineveh's Repentance (Jonah 3)
Yahweh's word comes to Jonah a second time; this time Jonah goes to Nineveh and proclaims that the
city will be overthrown in forty days. Remarkably, the Ninevites — from the king down to the least
of the people, and even the animals — believe God, fast, and put on sackcloth. God sees their
change of behavior and relents of the disaster he had said he would bring.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Jonah 3:1-2 (WEB): "Yahweh's word came to Jonah the second time, saying, 'Arise, go to Nineveh,
  that great city, and preach to it the message that I give you.'"
- Jonah 3:4 (WEB): "Jonah began to enter into the city a day's journey, and he cried out, and said,
  'In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!'"
- Jonah 3:5 (WEB): "The people of Nineveh believed God; and they proclaimed a fast and put on
  sackcloth, from their greatest even to their least."
- Jonah 3:10 (WEB): "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way. God relented of the
  disaster which he said he would do to them, and he didn't do it."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Jonah 3](https://ebible.org/web/JON03.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Jonah 3:1-5, 3:10](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+3&version=WEB)
— exact match, two independent sources.*

### 5. Jonah's Anger and the Plant (Jonah 4)
Nineveh's deliverance displeases Jonah greatly; he admits this is exactly why he fled in the first
place, since he knew Yahweh to be gracious and merciful. He sits outside the city, angry, waiting to
see what happens to it. Yahweh appoints a vine to shade him, which Jonah is glad of, then a worm to
destroy the vine and a scorching wind, which leaves Jonah faint and wishing to die. When Jonah
defends his anger over the lost vine, Yahweh closes the book with a question contrasting Jonah's
concern for a short-lived plant with Yahweh's own concern for Nineveh's many people (and animals).

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Jonah 4:2 (WEB): "He prayed to Yahweh, and said, 'Please, Yahweh, wasn't this what I said when I
  was still in my own country? Therefore I hurried to flee to Tarshish, for I knew that you are a
  gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of
  doing harm.'"
- Jonah 4:6 (WEB): "Yahweh God prepared a vine and made it to come up over Jonah, that it might be
  a shade over his head to deliver him from his discomfort. So Jonah was exceedingly glad because
  of the vine."
- Jonah 4:9 (WEB): "God said to Jonah, 'Is it right for you to be angry about the vine?' He said,
  'I am right to be angry, even to death.'"
- Jonah 4:10-11 (WEB): "Yahweh said, 'You have been concerned for the vine, for which you have not
  labored, neither made it grow; which came up in a night and perished in a night. Shouldn't I be
  concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand
  persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?'"

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Jonah 4](https://ebible.org/web/JON04.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Jonah 4](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Jonah+4&version=WEB)
— exact match, two independent sources, full chapter (verses 1-11) double-sourced given how load-bearing this closing scene is.*

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **The fish/"whale" and its historicity.** Whether the great fish of Jonah 1:17-2:10 is meant and
  read as a literal historical event or as a didactic/parabolic device is a genuine point of
  disagreement, including among mainstream evangelical scholars, and touches the same
  historicity-framing category (`CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §3) already excluded for MVP by precedent
  (e.g. Genesis's Flood-scope handling). This brief's bounding: no challenge item asks whether the
  fish event is historical, symbolic, or a parable — every fish-related challenge item (Scene 3)
  tests only what the text narrates happened (Jonah was swallowed, was inside three days and three
  nights, prayed, was vomited onto dry land), which is uncontested regardless of how a reader
  frames the event's historicity.
- **The "sign of Jonah" typology.** Later New Testament use of Jonah's story as a type/foreshadowing
  (explicitly out of scope for this OT-only product, per this task's own instructions) is not
  referenced anywhere in this brief. Flagged here only so the reviewer can confirm the omission is
  deliberate and complete, not an oversight — no scene, verse selection, or difficulty-ladder item
  alludes to or depends on that later reading.
- **Nineveh's repentance and Assyria's later history.** Nineveh's Assyrian empire is remembered
  elsewhere in the Old Testament (and in later Minor Prophets, e.g. Nahum) as a violent, ultimately
  unrepentant regional power that Israel had strong reason to fear and resent — some readers note a
  real narrative/theological tension between Jonah's account of citywide repentance and that later
  history. This brief's bounding: no challenge item asks a player to reconcile Jonah's account with
  Nahum's later material or to evaluate whether Nineveh's repentance was sincere, lasting, or
  historically verified — challenge items test only what Jonah 3 itself narrates (the proclamation,
  the response described, God's stated relenting), as a self-contained scene within this book.
- **God "relenting" (Jonah 3:10, 4:2) and divine immutability.** Some traditions read
  language of God "relenting" or "changing his mind" about a stated judgment as anthropomorphic
  narrative language describing a real change in outcome tied to human response, while others treat
  it as phenomenological language that doesn't imply any change in God's actual nature or
  foreknowledge — a live systematic-theology question in some traditions. This brief's bounding: no
  challenge item asks a player to explain *why* or *how* God relented, or to affirm a position on
  divine immutability — challenge items test only the narrated fact that Nineveh changed its
  behavior and that the disaster did not happen (3:10), and Jonah's own stated reason for having
  fled (4:2), both stated directly in the text.
- **Jonah's anger and the book's closing question (Jonah 4).** The book ends unresolved — Yahweh
  poses a question to Jonah and the text does not record his answer. This is not contested doctrine;
  it is the book's own deliberate literary structure, and this brief treats it as such: the
  difficulty ladder's capstone item (4:10-11) presents Yahweh's question as the text gives it,
  without supplying a resolution the book itself withholds. No challenge item invents or implies an
  answer on Jonah's behalf, and none asks a player to judge whether Jonah's anger was justified
  (moral-judgment territory, out of bounds per `CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §3) — only what Jonah said
  (4:9) and what Yahweh asked in response (4:10-11).
