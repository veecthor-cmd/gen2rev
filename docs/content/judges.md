## Setting / Era

Judges covers the period after Joshua's death and before Israel had a king — a repeated cycle
across roughly two centuries: Israel abandons Yahweh for other gods, is oppressed by a surrounding
power, cries out, and Yahweh raises up a "judge" (a military/deliverer figure, not primarily a
courtroom judge) who delivers them — until that judge dies and the cycle repeats, each iteration
described as worse than the last. The book closes on a note of moral and political disorder,
setting up the need for kingship that the Samuel and Kings worlds (9-12) resolve.

**A note on this brief's scope up front:** Judges contains roughly a dozen judges and several very
dark episodes. This brief deliberately covers only a general-audience-appropriate subset —
**Deborah and Gideon**, framed by the book's opening statement of the cycle and its closing verse.
Two of the book's most disturbing episodes are **deliberately excluded from challenge content, not
silently omitted**:

- **Jephthah's daughter (Judges 11)** — Jephthah's vow resulting in the death of his own daughter —
  is excluded as inappropriate for this game's target audience. It is not adapted, softened, or
  referenced in challenge content.
- **The Levite's concubine (Judges 19)** — the account of gang rape and murder that opens the book's
  closing section — is excluded for the same reason.

This is a content-appropriateness decision for the target audience, not a theological or
historicity judgment about those chapters — they remain part of the canonical text, just outside
this MVP's challenge-content scope. Other judges (e.g. Othniel, Ehud, Samson, Jephthah's military
role apart from the vow) are simply out of scope for this pass, no sensitivity implication intended
either way; a future content pass could revisit them.

## Pivotal Scenes (4)

### 1. The Cycle Begins (Judges 2:11-19)
Before naming any individual judge, the book states its own repeating pattern in the narrator's
voice: Israel turns to other gods, Yahweh hands them over to raiders, Yahweh raises up a judge who
delivers them, and when that judge dies, the people fall back into the same pattern, "more corrupt
than their fathers."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Judges 2:14 (WEB): "Yahweh's anger burned against Israel, and he delivered them into the hands of
  raiders who plundered them. He sold them into the hands of their enemies all around, so that they
  could no longer stand before their enemies."
- Judges 2:16 (WEB): "Yahweh raised up judges, who saved them out of the hand of those who plundered
  them."
- Judges 2:18-19 (WEB, trimmed): "When Yahweh raised up judges for them, then Yahweh was with the
  judge, and saved them out of the hand of their enemies all the days of the judge... But when the
  judge was dead, they turned back, and dealt more corruptly than their fathers..."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Judges 2](https://ebible.org/web/JDG02.htm).*

### 2. Deborah and Barak (Judges 4)
Deborah, a prophetess, is judging Israel. She summons Barak and relays Yahweh's command to fight
Sisera, commander of an oppressing king's army; Barak agrees only if Deborah goes with him. Yahweh
gives Israel victory; Sisera's army is routed.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Judges 4:4-5 (WEB): "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, judged Israel at that time.
  She lived under Deborah's palm tree between Ramah and Bethel in the hill country of Ephraim; and
  the children of Israel came up to her for judgment."
- Judges 4:6-7 (WEB, trimmed): "...Go and lead the way to Mount Tabor, and take with you ten
  thousand men of the children of Naphtali and of the children of Zebulun... I will draw to you, to
  the river Kishon, Sisera, the captain of Jabin's army... and I will deliver him into your hand."
- Judges 4:14-16 (WEB, trimmed): "Deborah said to Barak, 'Go; for this is the day in which Yahweh
  has delivered Sisera into your hand...' Yahweh confused Sisera, all his chariots, and all his
  army, with the edge of the sword before Barak... all the army of Sisera fell by the edge of the
  sword. There was not a man left."

**Deliberately excluded from challenge-ready content:** Judges 4:17-22 and 5:24-27, describing
Jael's killing of Sisera with a tent peg, are part of this chapter's text but are not selected as
challenge-ready verses — the graphic method of the killing is excluded for the same
target-audience reason as the Judges 11/19 exclusions above, even though this episode is far less
severe than those two. The scene description above stops at "the army fell," consistent with what
the text narrates about the battle's outcome without quoting the death-of-Sisera verses.

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Judges 4](https://ebible.org/web/JDG04.htm).*

### 3. Gideon and the Three Hundred (Judges 6-7)
Yahweh's angel calls Gideon, who doubts his own qualification; Gideon asks for a confirming sign
(a fleece test, twice) and receives it both times. Yahweh then instructs Gideon to reduce his army
from thousands to three hundred men "so that Israel doesn't brag." The three hundred rout the
Midianite camp using trumpets, torches, and shouting rather than close combat.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Judges 6:14-16 (WEB, trimmed): "Yahweh looked at him, and said, 'Go in this your might, and save
  Israel from the hand of Midian. Haven't I sent you?' He said to him, 'O Lord, how shall I save
  Israel? Behold, my family is the poorest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my father's
  house.' Yahweh said to him, 'Surely I will be with you, and you shall strike the Midianites as
  one man.'"
- Judges 6:37 (WEB): "...behold, I will put a fleece of wool on the threshing floor. If there is
  dew on the fleece only, and it is dry on all the ground, then I'll know that you will save Israel
  by my hand, as you have spoken."
- Judges 7:2 (WEB): "The people who are with you are too many for me to give the Midianites into
  their hand, lest Israel brag against me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'"
- Judges 7:20-21 (WEB, trimmed): "...they shouted, 'The sword of Yahweh and of Gideon!' They each
  stood in his place around the camp, and all the army ran; and they shouted, and put them to
  flight."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Judges 6](https://ebible.org/web/JDG06.htm), [ebible.org WEB, Judges 7](https://ebible.org/web/JDG07.htm).*

### 4. The Cycle Ends — For Now (Judges 21:25)
The book's final verse states plainly why things had gone the way they had: no king, everyone doing
what was right in their own eyes. This closing line ties forward directly to the Samuel and Kings
worlds (9-12), where Israel asks for and receives a king.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Judges 21:25 (WEB): "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right
  in his own eyes."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Judges 21](https://ebible.org/web/JDG21.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Judges 21:25](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Judges+21%3A25&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **Explicit content exclusions (stated plainly, not silently omitted).** Jephthah's daughter
  (Judges 11) and the Levite's concubine (Judges 19) are excluded from this brief's challenge
  content as inappropriate for the target audience, per this document's Setting/Era section above.
  This is flagged here so the reviewer evaluates the exclusion decision deliberately (is the
  boundary drawn in the right place, is anything adjacent to these chapters accidentally still
  included) rather than it surfacing as a surprise.
- **Jael's killing of Sisera (Judges 4:17-22, 5:24-27).** Excluded from challenge-ready verse text
  for its graphic method (see Scene 2 above); the surrounding battle narrative (Deborah's role,
  Barak's reluctance, the army's defeat) remains in-bounds. Flagged so the reviewer confirms the
  line was drawn in a defensible place — this episode is far less severe than the two full-chapter
  exclusions above, so it's handled as a verse-level exclusion within an otherwise-included scene
  rather than cutting the whole Deborah story.
- **Deborah as a female judge/prophet and military leader.** This is a live, real disagreement in
  contemporary Christian discussion: egalitarian readings point to Deborah as an uncontested,
  unqualified example of a woman in top civil, spiritual, and military leadership in Israel
  (Judges 4:4-5 records no apology or qualification for her role); complementarian readings
  generally treat her as a rare Old Testament exception not intended to establish a pattern for
  church-age leadership, sometimes reading Barak's reluctance as reflecting poorly on the men of
  the period rather than on Deborah. This brief's bounding: challenge items test only that Deborah
  judged Israel, was a prophetess, and summoned Barak — narrative fact, direct from 4:4-5 — never
  whether her example settles a present-day leadership question. **Structural rule (added per
  theological review, `docs/reviews/judges-review.md`): Judges 4:4-5 challenge items are restricted
  to recall/fill-in-blank or narrative-sequence format only — never multiple-choice framed around
  what her example implies for a present-day leadership question.**
- **Gideon's fleece test — faith or doubt?** Commentators genuinely diverge on how to read Gideon
  twice asking for a confirming sign after already being told directly by Yahweh's messenger that he
  would succeed: some treat it as understandable, humanly sympathetic doubt that Yahweh patiently
  accommodates; others read it as a violation of the later principle "you shall not put Yahweh your
  God to the test" (Deuteronomy 6:16) and evidence of weak faith. This brief's bounding: the
  challenge item (6:37) tests only that Gideon proposed the fleece test and what the test was, never
  whether the test was righteous or sinful — no challenge item asks a player to judge Gideon's
  faith. **Structural rule (added per theological review, `docs/reviews/judges-review.md`): Judges
  6:37 challenge items are restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only — never multiple-choice
  framed around whether the test was righteous, doubtful, or otherwise evaluated.**
- **The judges' cycle as a "cycle of decline" framing.** The book's own narrator states this pattern
  explicitly (2:11-19) — this is direct textual content, not later interpretation, so no
  cross-tradition check was needed for the framing itself. Noted here for completeness per the style
  guide's requirement that every brief have this section, even where the answer is "text states this
  directly, no genuine disagreement found."
