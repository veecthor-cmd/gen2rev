## Setting / Era

Esther is set roughly a century after the Babylonian exile, in the Persian royal court at Susa,
during the reign of King Ahasuerus (widely identified with Xerxes I, r. 486–465 BC). Unlike every
other book built so far in this project, Esther is a complete, self-contained narrative rather than
a slice of a longer national history — one court drama, start to finish, with no sequel and no
earlier installment. A Jewish orphan girl, Esther, raised by her cousin Mordecai, becomes queen of
Persia without disclosing her ethnicity; when the king's chief official, Haman, engineers a
kingdom-wide decree to annihilate the Jewish people after Mordecai refuses to bow to him, Esther
risks her life to intervene, the decree is reversed, and the deliverance is commemorated permanently
in the festival of Purim — which Jewish communities still keep today, making this one of the few
books in the project whose ending event has a continuous, present-day observance attached to it.

**A note on the text worth stating plainly, not treating as a problem to paper over:** Esther is one
of only two books in the Hebrew Bible (the other is Song of Solomon) that never once names God,
uses a divine title, or narrates a prayer, miracle, or direct divine act. Every reversal in the
story — Esther's rise, the discovery of a plot against the king, Haman's fall, the Jews' deliverance
— happens through human decisions, timing, and coincidence, narrated without commentary on divine
causation. This is a genuine, well-documented textual feature (see Contested Territory below for
how both Jewish and Christian tradition read it), not an oversight of this brief.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. Esther Becomes Queen (Esther 2)
After Queen Vashti is deposed for refusing the king's summons (Esther 1 — not built as a separate
scene here; referenced only as context for why the queenship was open), the king's officials gather
young women from across the empire as candidates, among them Esther, whom her older cousin Mordecai
has raised as his own daughter after the death of her parents. Esther conceals her Jewish identity
on Mordecai's instruction. She pleases the king and is crowned queen in his place of Vashti.

**Handling note:** this scene includes the royal-harem/gathering-of-candidates context. Per this
brief's bounding (see Contested Territory), it is presented as a factual account of what happens to
Esther — she is taken to the palace, placed in the king's household, and made queen — with no
romanticized or salacious framing, and no challenge item dwells on this context beyond the narrative
facts below.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Esther 2:7 (WEB, trimmed): "He brought up Hadassah, that is, Esther, his uncle's daughter; for
  she had neither father nor mother. The maiden was fair and beautiful; and when her father and
  mother were dead, Mordecai took her for his own daughter."
- Esther 2:15 (WEB, trimmed): "Now when the turn of Esther... came to go in to the king, she
  required nothing but what Hegai the king's eunuch, the keeper of the women, advised. Esther
  obtained favor in the sight of all those who looked at her."
- Esther 2:17 (WEB): "The king loved Esther more than all the women, and she obtained favor and
  kindness in his sight more than all the virgins; so that he set the royal crown on her head, and
  made her queen instead of Vashti."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Esther 2](https://ebible.org/web/EST02.htm).*

### 2. Haman's Plot — The Decree Against the Jews (Esther 3)
Haman, elevated to the king's highest office, is enraged when Mordecai refuses to bow to him.
Learning Mordecai is a Jew, Haman resolves not merely to punish Mordecai but to destroy the entire
Jewish people throughout the empire. He secures the king's authorization and a kingdom-wide decree
is issued, setting a date for the annihilation of all Jews, "both young and old, little children and
women, in one day."

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Esther 3:5-6 (WEB, trimmed): "When Haman saw that Mordecai didn't bow down nor pay him homage,
  Haman was full of wrath... Haman sought to destroy all the Jews who were throughout the whole
  kingdom of Ahasuerus, even Mordecai's people."
- Esther 3:9 (WEB, trimmed): "'If it pleases the king, let it be written that they be destroyed;
  and I will pay ten thousand talents of silver into the hands of those who are in charge of the
  king's business...'"
- Esther 3:13 (WEB, trimmed): "Letters were sent by couriers into all the king's provinces, to
  destroy, to kill, and to cause to perish, all Jews, both young and old, little children and
  women, in one day, even on the thirteenth day of the twelfth month, which is the month Adar, and
  to plunder their possessions."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Esther 3](https://ebible.org/web/EST03.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Esther 3:8-13](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+3%3A8-13&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

### 3. "For Such a Time as This" — Esther's Decision (Esther 4)
Mordecai learns of the decree and urges Esther to approach the king on behalf of her people — a
request that carries real danger, since approaching the king uninvited was punishable by death.
Mordecai's appeal, and Esther's response committing to act regardless of personal risk, is the
book's best-known passage and its narrative and moral turning point.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Esther 4:13-14 (WEB): "Don't think to yourself that you will escape in the king's house any more
  than all the Jews. For if you remain silent now, then relief and deliverance will come to the
  Jews from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. Who knows if you haven't
  come to the kingdom for such a time as this?"
- Esther 4:16 (WEB): "Go, gather together all the Jews who are present in Susa, and fast for me,
  and neither eat nor drink three days, night or day. I and my maidens will also fast the same way.
  Then I will go in to the king, which is against the law; and if I perish, I perish."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Esther 4](https://ebible.org/web/EST04.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Esther 4:13-16](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+4%3A13-16&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

### 4. Esther's Banquets and Haman's Downfall (Esther 5-7)
Esther approaches the king unsummoned and is received; she invites the king and Haman to two
banquets. Haman, meanwhile, has gallows built for Mordecai. At the second banquet Esther reveals her
Jewish identity and exposes Haman's plot against her and her people. The king's anger turns on
Haman, who is hanged on the very gallows he built for Mordecai.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Esther 7:3-4 (WEB, trimmed): "Esther the queen answered, 'If I have found favor in your sight, O
  king, and if it pleases the king, let my life be given me at my petition, and my people at my
  request. For we are sold, I and my people, to be destroyed, to be slain, and to perish...'"
- Esther 7:9-10 (WEB, trimmed): "Harbonah... said, 'Behold, the gallows fifty cubits high, which
  Haman has made for Mordecai... is standing at Haman's house.' The king said, 'Hang him on it!' So
  they hanged Haman on the gallows that he had prepared for Mordecai..."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Esther 7](https://ebible.org/web/EST07.htm).*

### 5. Reversal and the Establishment of Purim (Esther 8-9)
The original decree cannot be revoked (Persian law), so the king issues a counter-decree allowing
the Jews to defend themselves on the appointed day. When the day comes, the Jews prevail against
those who attack them. Mordecai and Esther then establish an annual two-day festival, Purim, so the
deliverance is remembered every year — an institution still kept in Jewish communities today. This
is the book's capstone and the natural boss-battle beat for this world.

**Handling note (see Contested Territory):** consistent with this project's precedent on 2 Kings'
darker chapters, this scene is built at the narrative-summary level — what happened, in what order —
not at the level of graphic detail. **Explicitly excluded from challenge-ready content:** Esther
9:6-10 (the killing of Haman's ten sons, named individually) and Esther 9:16's casualty figure
(75,000) are not selected for any challenge item or scene description in this brief, flagged here so
they are not silently added later without review.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Esther 9:22 (WEB, trimmed): "...the days in which the Jews had rest from their enemies, and the
  month which was turned to them from sorrow to gladness, and from mourning into a holiday; that
  they should make them days of feasting and gladness, and of sending presents of food to one
  another, and gifts to the needy."
- Esther 9:26 (WEB, trimmed): "Therefore they called these days 'Purim,' from the word 'Pur.'"
- Esther 9:28 (WEB, trimmed): "...these days should be remembered and kept throughout every
  generation, every family, every province, and every city; and that these days of Purim should not
  fail from among the Jews, nor their memory perish from their offspring."

*Source: [ebible.org WEB, Esther 9](https://ebible.org/web/EST09.htm), cross-checked against
[biblegateway.com WEB, Esther 9:20-28](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Esther+9%3A20-28&version=WEB) — exact match, two independent sources.*

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **The absence of God's name from the text.** As noted above, Esther never names God directly.
  This has real cross-tradition weight: Jewish tradition largely reads it as a deliberate literary
  and theological device — divine providence operating "hidden" (the name Esther is traditionally
  linked to the Hebrew root for "hidden," *hastir*) rather than absent, visible only to those who
  read the story's coincidences rightly. Mainstream evangelical Christian sources converge on a
  similar "hidden providence" reading. Separately, the book's canonical status was historically
  debated in Christian tradition specifically because of this feature — most notably Martin Luther's
  documented discomfort with Esther, though this is a canonicity debate about the whole book, not a
  disagreement about what any individual verse says. **This brief's bounding:** the brief states the
  observable textual fact (God is not named) as a narrative/literary feature worth noting, and no
  challenge item asserts or requires a position on *why* the author omitted God's name, whether
  providence is really at work, or the book's canonicity. That interpretive question is out of
  bounds per the style guide's doctrinal-interpretation exclusion; the fact that the name doesn't
  appear is safely in-bounds as a plain textual observation if a future challenge-authoring pass
  wants to use it (this brief does not build a graded item on it either way, to stay conservative).
- **The "gathering of virgins" / royal harem context (Esther 2).** Real sensitivity, per the
  assignment's own guidance: this must be handled factually, with no romanticizing or salacious
  framing. Jewish rabbinic/midrashic tradition specifically addresses the ethical discomfort here by
  reading Esther as compelled rather than a willing participant, and as maintaining her protected
  status through passive, non-consenting resistance to the situation — a reading that reinforces
  (rather than complicates) this brief's non-romanticized framing. **This brief's bounding:**
  challenge items test only that Esther was taken into the king's household and became queen
  (2:7, 2:15, 2:17) — plain narrative fact — never anything about the selection process itself, and
  no challenge item frames this scene as a romance. **Structural rule (added per theological
  review, `docs/reviews/esther-review.md`): Esther 2:7, 2:15, and 2:17 challenge items are
  restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only — never multiple-choice — so a future
  challenge-authoring pass can't accidentally introduce distractor options that read as evaluating
  or romanticizing the selection process.**
- **The genocide plot (ch. 3) and its violent reversal (ch. 8-9).** Real sensitivity, per the
  assignment's guidance and this project's 2 Kings precedent. Classic cross-tradition commentary
  (Matthew Henry, Barnes, Benson, Gill, Keil and Delitzsch — surveyed via Bible Hub's aggregation)
  converges on reading the Jews' actions in chapter 9 as authorized self-defense under the king's
  counter-decree, not unprovoked aggression — the text itself repeatedly notes the Jews took no
  plunder even though the decree permitted it. That said, some modern scholarship and even Luther's
  own commentary describe the chapter's violence as troubling ("bloodthirsty," in Luther's words),
  and the casualty figures are large. **This brief's bounding, matching 2 Kings' precedent:** the
  genocide plot and its reversal are stated at the narrative-summary level (what happened, in what
  order, that the Jews prevailed and were delivered) — never at graphic-detail level. Esther
  9:6-10 (Haman's ten sons named individually as killed) and the 9:16 casualty count are explicitly
  excluded from challenge-ready content, matching how 2 Kings excluded its most graphic passages.
  No challenge item asks a player to evaluate whether the violence was justified — only what the
  text narrates happened.
