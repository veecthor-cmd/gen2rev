## Setting / Era

Exodus picks up where Genesis left off: the family of Israel that settled in Egypt under Joseph's
protection has grown into a large people, now enslaved by a Pharaoh "who didn't know Joseph."
World 2 covers Israel's escape from slavery in Egypt through the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai
and the covenant's near-collapse at the golden calf — the book that establishes Israel as a
covenant nation with Yahweh, not just a family. Unlike Genesis, Exodus is tightly plotted around a
single arc (deliverance → covenant), which makes scene selection easier than it will be for
Leviticus (World 3), where the material is mostly legal/ritual rather than narrative — see that
book's brief for how the format is adapted there. No historicity/dating claims (e.g. date of the
Exodus, identity of "Pharaoh") are made or implied by this brief; see Contested Territory below.

## Pivotal Scenes (5)

### 1. Moses' Call at the Burning Bush (Exodus 3)
Moses, tending sheep in Midian, encounters God in a bush that burns without being consumed. God
identifies himself, commissions Moses to confront Pharaoh, and reveals his name.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Exodus 3:5 (WEB): "He said, 'Don't come close. Take off your sandals, for the place you are
  standing on is holy ground.'"
- Exodus 3:7-8 (WEB, trimmed): "Yahweh said, 'I have surely seen the affliction of my people who
  are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters, for I know their sorrows. I
  have come down to deliver them out of the hand of the Egyptians, and to bring them up out of
  that land to a good and large land, to a land flowing with milk and honey.'"
- Exodus 3:14 (WEB): "God said to Moses, 'I AM WHO I AM,' and he said, 'You shall tell the children
  of Israel this: "I AM has sent me to you."'"

### 2. The Plagues and the Passover (Exodus 7-12)
A series of plagues strikes Egypt after Pharaoh repeatedly refuses to let Israel go; the tenth and
final plague — against Egypt's firstborn — is preceded by God's instructions for the Passover meal,
which Israel keeps as protection and later as an annual memorial.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Exodus 12:12-13 (WEB): "'For I will go through the land of Egypt in that night, and will strike
  all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, both man and animal. I will execute judgments against
  all the gods of Egypt. I am Yahweh. The blood shall be to you for a token on the houses where
  you are. When I see the blood, I will pass over you, and no plague will be on you to destroy you
  when I strike the land of Egypt.'"
- Exodus 12:14 (WEB): "'This day shall be a memorial for you. You shall keep it as a feast to
  Yahweh. You shall keep it as a feast throughout your generations by an ordinance forever.'"
- Exodus 12:29-30 (WEB): "At midnight, Yahweh struck all the firstborn in the land of Egypt, from
  the firstborn of Pharaoh who sat on his throne to the firstborn of the captive who was in the
  dungeon, and all the firstborn of livestock. Pharaoh rose up in the night, he, and all his
  servants, and all the Egyptians; and there was a great cry in Egypt, for there was not a house
  where there was not one dead." *(Handled per the sensitivity note below — see Contested
  Territory.)*

### 3. The Crossing of the Red Sea (Exodus 14)
With Pharaoh's army pursuing, Moses stretches his hand over the sea; it divides, Israel crosses on
dry ground, and the pursuing army is drowned when the waters return.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Exodus 14:21-22 (WEB): "Moses stretched out his hand over the sea, and Yahweh caused the sea to
  go back by a strong east wind all night, and made the sea dry land, and the waters were divided.
  The children of Israel went into the middle of the sea on the dry ground; and the waters were a
  wall to them on their right hand and on their left."
- Exodus 14:30-31 (WEB, trimmed): "Thus Yahweh saved Israel that day out of the hand of the
  Egyptians... Israel saw the great work which Yahweh did to the Egyptians, and the people feared
  Yahweh; and they believed in Yahweh and in his servant Moses."

### 4. Sinai: Covenant and the Ten Commandments (Exodus 19-20)
God proposes a covenant with Israel at Sinai — they will be his "kingdom of priests and a holy
nation" if they keep it — and the people agree. God then speaks the Ten Commandments directly to
the people.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Exodus 19:5-6 (WEB, trimmed): "'Now therefore, if you will indeed obey my voice and keep my
  covenant, then you shall be my own possession from among all peoples; for all the earth is mine;
  and you shall be to me a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.'"
- Exodus 19:8 (WEB): "All the people answered together, and said, 'All that Yahweh has spoken we
  will do.'"
- Exodus 20:2-3 (WEB): "'I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of
  the house of bondage. You shall have no other gods before me.'"
- Exodus 20:12-15 (WEB): "'Honor your father and your mother, that your days may be long in the
  land which Yahweh your God gives you. You shall not murder. You shall not commit adultery. You
  shall not steal.'"

### 5. The Golden Calf (Exodus 32)
While Moses is on the mountain, the people pressure Aaron into making a golden calf to worship.
Moses returns, breaks the stone tablets in anger, destroys the calf, and intercedes with God on
the people's behalf.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- Exodus 32:1 (WEB, trimmed): "The people gathered themselves together to Aaron, and said to him,
  'Come, make us gods, which shall go before us; for as for this Moses, the man who brought us up
  out of the land of Egypt, we don't know what has become of him.'"
- Exodus 32:19-20 (WEB): "As soon as he came near to the camp, he saw the calf and the dancing.
  Then Moses' anger grew hot, and he threw the tablets out of his hands, and broke them beneath
  the mountain. He took the calf which they had made, and burned it with fire, ground it to
  powder, and scattered it on the water, and made the children of Israel drink it."
- Exodus 32:31-32 (WEB): "Moses returned to Yahweh, and said, 'Oh, this people have sinned a great
  sin, and have made themselves gods of gold. Yet now, if you will, forgive their sin—and if not,
  please blot me out of your book which you have written.'"

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 — flagged for the theological reviewer, not resolved here:

- **The tenth plague / death of Egypt's firstborn (Exodus 12:29-30).** Per this assignment's
  sensitivity note: this is the one moment in the book with real graphic-content potential. The
  challenge-ready verses above are the plain WEB narration (no added detail, no dwelling on
  suffering), and no challenge item asks a player to visualize, describe, or elaborate on the
  event beyond what the cited text states. **Structural rule (added per theological review,
  `docs/reviews/exodus-review.md`): Exodus 12:29-30 may only be used in recall/fill-in-blank or
  strict sequence-order format — never a multiple-choice or "describe what happened" format that
  invites elaboration — and should not be the first item a player sees in this scene's challenge
  set (sequence it after the Passover-institution verses, 12:12-14, so the plague isn't the
  player's first exposure to this beat).**
- **The hardening of Pharaoh's heart.** Exodus alternates between saying Yahweh hardens Pharaoh's
  heart (e.g. Ex 4:21, 7:3, 14:4) and saying Pharaoh hardens his own heart (e.g. Ex 8:15, 8:32,
  9:34) — a textual pattern that sits directly on top of the live Christian theological debate over
  divine sovereignty vs. human free will (broadly, Reformed/Calvinist vs. Arminian/Wesleyan
  readings), and Jewish tradition has its own distinct treatment of the same tension — Maimonides
  reads the hardening as a *punishment* that withdrew Pharaoh's capacity to repent after he had
  already sinned freely, while other rabbinic voices (Albo, Sforno) read it as *restoring* his free
  will so his eventual refusal would be genuinely his own. **Structural rule: this brief does not
  select any "hardening" verse (e.g. Ex 4:21, 7:3, 8:15, 9:12, 14:4, 14:17) as a challenge-ready
  verse anywhere in this brief**, precisely to keep the hardening motif out of graded content
  altogether rather than trying to bound a single verse narratively. If a future challenge-authoring
  pass wants to add one, it must stay strictly on *that* the hardening happened and *when* in the
  sequence, never *why* or *how* it happened or which tradition's reading is correct.
- **The divine name (Exodus 3:14-15).** WEB renders the Tetragrammaton as "Yahweh" throughout,
  following the translation's own stated convention — this is a translation choice, not a
  theological claim by this brief. Jewish tradition does not pronounce the divine name aloud in
  liturgical contexts; some readers may find a game asking them to "recall" or type "Yahweh"
  sensitive for that reason. Recommend the reviewer weigh in on whether recall-format items using
  "Yahweh" (as opposed to items that only ask about "I AM WHO I AM," which is the text's own
  self-designation) need any special handling.
- **Numbering of the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1-17).** Jewish, Catholic/Lutheran, and
  Reformed/Orthodox traditions number the Decalogue differently (e.g. whether "I am Yahweh your
  God" is commandment 1 or a preamble, whether the prohibition on idols is folded into the
  first commandment or stands alone, how the coveting clauses are split). No challenge item in
  this brief numbers the commandments or asks "which commandment is #3" — challenges test content,
  order-of-appearance in the text, and verbatim recall only, never a numbering scheme, precisely to
  avoid taking a side in this cross-tradition difference.
- **The golden calf and Aaron's culpability (Exodus 32).** The text narrates Aaron making the calf
  under public pressure, then later blaming the people (Ex 32:22-24) — later tradition debates how
  culpable Aaron was and how the episode fits with his priestly role established a few chapters
  earlier. Challenge items here test only what happens (the people asked, Aaron made the calf,
  Moses broke the tablets, Moses interceded), never an evaluation of Aaron's guilt or character.
