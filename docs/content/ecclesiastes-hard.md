## Design intent for this tier

Per Kachi: "hard... needs very deep scripture knowledge." This tier draws on verses that are
narratively or thematically significant but less likely to be immediately recognized — precise
phrasing from inside the "time for everything" poem, the book's harsher observations on wisdom and
labor, and the aging-and-death allegory that immediately follows the book's closing counsel — and
requires typed recall (no multiple-choice scaffolding) wherever the format allows. It stays inside
the same guardrails as every other tier: verbatim WEB text only, narrative fact only, no doctrinal
position required.

## Contested Territory

Everything already flagged in `docs/content/ecclesiastes.md` applies unchanged, including the
resolved-hope-vs-bleak structural rule (not engaged by any item in this brief — no item here combines
Passage 1 and Passage 4 material at all, pure ordering or otherwise).

**New for this tier — reviewed against live cross-tradition sources in
`docs/reviews/ecclesiastes-hard-review.md` §3, bounding confirmed to hold:**

- **Ecclesiastes 3:11, "He has also set eternity in their hearts."** (item 4) The Hebrew *olam*,
  which WEB renders "eternity," is a genuine, live translation-level divergence distinct from the
  "hevel"/vanity question already flagged at the whole-book level: KJV renders the same clause "the
  world in their heart," while NIV/ESV/NASB/NET follow WEB's "eternity" — reflecting real
  disagreement over whether the word points to unending time, an undefined "long duration," or (per
  the KJV tradition) the created order itself. **Structural rule: this item is recall-only
  (fill-in-blank on "eternity"), never multiple-choice; no challenge in any tier may ask what the
  clause means or whether "eternity" is the correct rendering.** `docs/content/ecclesiastes-easy.md`
  item 8 displays but does not grade the same clause, which trivially satisfies this rule; that
  brief's own structural-rule wording was corrected during theological review (see
  `docs/reviews/ecclesiastes-easy-review.md` §3) to state this recall-only bounding explicitly,
  since this brief is the first to actually grade the word and its original absolute "never grade in
  any tier" wording would otherwise have contradicted this item.
- **Ecclesiastes 12:6's aging-and-death imagery** ("the silver cord," "the golden bowl," "the pitcher
  broken at the spring," "the wheel broken at the cistern") is traditionally read by many commentators
  as an extended body-allegory (e.g. the spinal cord, the skull, circulatory function) describing the
  approach of death. **No challenge item asks the player to identify or affirm any part of this
  allegorical mapping** — every item stays at direct-quotation/sequencing level, testing only that the
  text states these four images in this order, which is uncontested across traditions.
- **No new contested ground otherwise** — 1:14, 1:18, 3:7, 4:9, 4:10, and 12:1 (evil days come) are
  plain narrative/reflective text with no live cross-tradition disagreement found.
