## Setting / Era

John is the fourth Gospel and, per traditional Christian dating, the last written of the four —
composed, per most conservative and critical estimates alike, toward the end of the first century
AD, addressed to an audience already familiar with the basic Gospel story. Structurally and
theologically, John stands apart from Matthew, Mark, and Luke (the "synoptic" Gospels, so called
because their shared material lets them be viewed "together"): roughly ninety percent of John's
content has no direct parallel in the other three. Where the synoptics narrate a broad sweep of
Galilean ministry through short episodes and parables, John selects a much smaller number of
extended episodes — the text itself calls them "signs" (Greek *sēmeia*) rather than merely
"miracles," explicitly framing them as pointing toward who Jesus is (John 20:30-31) — and pairs
several of them with long discourses, especially seven distinctive "I am" statements (bread of
life, light of the world, the door, the good shepherd, the resurrection and the life, the way/
truth/life, the true vine) that appear nowhere in this exact form in the other Gospels. John also
alone contains an extended Farewell Discourse (chapters 14-17, Jesus's final teaching to his
disciples the night before his crucifixion, closing with an extended prayer) and the resurrection
appearance to Thomas that gives us the phrase "doubting Thomas." This brief is built to foreground
exactly this distinctive material — the Prologue, the sign-narratives largely unique to John, the
"I am" statements, and the Farewell Discourse — while still carrying a complete narrative arc from
the Word's eternal existence (1:1) through the crucifixion and resurrection to the book's own
stated purpose statement (20:30-31) and epilogue (21).

**As one of four Gospel worlds played back-to-back (Matthew 40, Mark 41, Luke 42, John 43), this
brief deliberately does not re-tread the "standard highlights" shared across all four** (the
baptism, the feeding of the 5,000 as a generic miracle beat, a generic parable set, a generic
Last-Supper/Gethsemane/trial/crucifixion sequence). Where John narrates an event the synoptics also
cover, this brief either skips it (no birth narrative — John has none; no Sermon-on-the-Mount-style
parable teaching — John has almost no parables at all) or selects the material and phrasing that is
distinctively John's own telling (e.g. the crucifixion beats selected below are the ones unique to
John's account — the spear-thrust and blood-and-water detail, not a generic "he was crucified"
recap).

## Pivotal Scenes (14)

### 1. The Prologue: The Word Made Flesh (John 1:1-18)
John opens not with a birth narrative but with a cosmic prologue: the Word (Greek *Logos*) existed
"in the beginning," was with God, and *was* God; all things were made through him; and the Word
"became flesh and lived among us." John the Baptist is introduced as a witness to the light, not
the light itself. See Contested Territory — this is the single densest Christological passage in
the book and is handled per the narrative-fact/direct-quote discipline throughout.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 1:1 (WEB): "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was
  God."
- John 1:3 (WEB): "All things were made through him. Without him, nothing was made that has been
  made."
- John 1:14 (WEB): "The Word became flesh and lived among us. We saw his glory, such glory as of
  the only born Son of the Father, full of grace and truth."
- John 1:29 (WEB): "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" *(John the
  Baptist's words about Jesus.)*

### 2. The First Sign: Water into Wine at Cana (John 2:1-11)
At a wedding in Cana, Jesus's mother tells him the wine has run out; Jesus has six stone water pots
filled with water, which becomes wine when drawn out and tasted by the ruler of the feast. The text
explicitly calls this "the beginning of his signs" and states it revealed his glory, prompting his
disciples to believe.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 2:3 (WEB): "When the wine ran out, Jesus' mother said to him, 'They have no wine.'"
- John 2:7-8 (WEB, trimmed): "Jesus said to them, 'Fill the water pots with water.' So they filled
  them up to the brim. He said to them, 'Now draw some out, and take it to the ruler of the
  feast.'"
- John 2:11 (WEB): "This beginning of his signs Jesus did in Cana of Galilee, and revealed his
  glory; and his disciples believed in him."

### 3. Nicodemus and Being "Born Anew" (John 3:1-17)
Nicodemus, a Pharisee and ruler of the Jews, comes to Jesus by night; Jesus tells him no one can
see God's Kingdom without being "born anew" / "born of water and Spirit." The scene is the source
of the book's best-known single verse, John 3:16, and its statement that God's purpose in sending
the Son was to save the world, not to judge it.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 3:3 (WEB): "Jesus answered him, 'Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born anew, he
  can't see God's Kingdom.'"
- John 3:16 (WEB): "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever
  believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life."
- John 3:17 (WEB): "For God didn't send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the
  world should be saved through him."

### 4. The Samaritan Woman at the Well (John 4:1-42)
Jesus, resting at Jacob's well in Samaria, asks a Samaritan woman for water and offers her "living
water" that becomes "a well of water springing up to eternal life." She raises the coming Messiah;
Jesus states plainly, "I am he, the one who speaks to you." She returns to her city and many
Samaritans believe because of her testimony and then because of Jesus's own words — a scene unique
to John, notable for crossing the era's Jewish-Samaritan social divide and for one of Jesus's most
direct self-identifications as Messiah in any Gospel.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 4:9 (WEB): "The Samaritan woman therefore said to him, 'How is it that you, being a Jew, ask
  for a drink from me, a Samaritan woman?'"
- John 4:14 (WEB): "But whoever drinks of the water that I will give him will never thirst again;
  but the water that I will give him will become in him a well of water springing up to eternal
  life."
- John 4:25-26 (WEB): "The woman said to him, 'I know that Messiah is coming, he who is called
  Christ.' Jesus said to her, 'I am he, the one who speaks to you.'"
- John 4:42 (WEB, trimmed): "They said to the woman, 'Now we believe... we have heard for
  ourselves, and know that this is indeed the Christ, the Savior of the world.'"

### 5. Feeding the Five Thousand and Walking on Water (John 6:1-21)
Jesus feeds a crowd of about 5,000 from a boy's five barley loaves and two fish, with twelve
baskets of leftovers gathered afterward; that night he walks on the sea toward his disciples' boat
and tells them, "I am he. Don't be afraid." (This event also appears in the synoptics; it is
included here briefly because it sets up John's uniquely extended discourse that follows — the
"I am the bread of life" teaching — which is the material this brief actually emphasizes.)

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 6:11 (WEB, trimmed): "Jesus took the loaves, and having given thanks, he distributed to the
  disciples, and the disciples to those who were sitting down, likewise also of the fish as much as
  they desired."
- John 6:13 (WEB): "So they gathered them up, and filled twelve baskets with broken pieces from the
  five barley loaves, which were left over by those who had eaten."
- John 6:20 (WEB): "But he said to them, 'It is I. Don't be afraid.'"
- John 6:35 (WEB): "Jesus said to them, 'I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will not be
  hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.'"

### 6. "I Am the Light of the World" and "The Truth Will Make You Free" (John 8:12, 31-32)
In the temple, Jesus declares himself "the light of the world," promising that whoever follows him
will not walk in darkness. Later in the same chapter he tells believing Jews that if they remain in
his word, they will know the truth, and the truth will set them free — one of the most widely
quoted lines in the book, independent of the disputed passage that precedes it in some manuscripts
(see Contested Territory: John 7:53-8:11).

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 8:12 (WEB): "Again, therefore, Jesus spoke to them, saying, 'I am the light of the world. He
  who follows me will not walk in the darkness, but will have the light of life.'"
- John 8:31-32 (WEB): "Jesus therefore said to those Jews who had believed him, 'If you remain in
  my word, then you are truly my disciples. You will know the truth, and the truth will make you
  free.'"

### 7. "Before Abraham Was, I Am" (John 8:58)
Later in the same temple exchange, Jesus makes one of his most direct self-identifications in the
book: "before Abraham came into existence, I AM" — language widely understood (see Contested
Territory) as echoing the divine name revealed to Moses at the burning bush (Exodus 3:14). This
brief includes the verse itself and its plain content (a claim to exist before Abraham) without
building any challenge item that requires affirming a specific historical-theological account of
what the claim means.

**Challenge-ready verse:**
- John 8:58 (WEB): "Jesus said to them, 'Most certainly, I tell you, before Abraham came into
  existence, I AM.'"

### 8. The Man Born Blind (John 9:1-25)
Jesus heals a man born blind by making mud with saliva and sending him to wash in the pool of
Siloam; the man returns seeing. Asked by his disciples whether the man's blindness was punishment
for his own sin or his parents', Jesus states plainly that neither was the cause, but that God's
works might be revealed in him. The chapter's second half (a lengthy interrogation by religious
authorities, not selected for challenge content — see Contested Territory) closes with the healed
man's own simple testimony.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 9:2-3 (WEB): "His disciples asked him, 'Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he
  was born blind?' Jesus answered, 'This man didn't sin, nor did his parents, but that the works of
  God might be revealed in him.'"
- John 9:7 (WEB, trimmed): "'Go, wash in the pool of Siloam' (which means 'Sent'). So he went away,
  washed, and came back seeing."
- John 9:25 (WEB): "One thing I do know: that though I was blind, now I see."

### 9. "I Am the Good Shepherd" (John 10:1-30)
Jesus describes himself as the good shepherd who lays down his life for the sheep, in contrast to a
thief who comes only to steal and destroy. He states that his sheep hear his voice, that he gives
them eternal life, that no one can snatch them from his hand, and closes the discourse with "I and
the Father are one" — the second of the book's most direct claims of unity with the Father (see
Contested Territory, same handling as 8:58).

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 10:10-11 (WEB): "The thief only comes to steal, kill, and destroy. I came that they may have
  life, and may have it abundantly. I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life
  for the sheep."
- John 10:27-28 (WEB): "My sheep hear my voice, and I know them, and they follow me. I give eternal
  life to them. They will never perish, and no one will snatch them out of my hand."
- John 10:30 (WEB): "I and the Father are one."

### 10. Raising Lazarus: "I Am the Resurrection and the Life" (John 11:1-44)
Jesus's friend Lazarus dies; arriving after four days, Jesus tells Lazarus's sister Martha, "I am
the resurrection and the life," then weeps at the tomb (John 11:35, "Jesus wept," the Bible's
shortest verse in most English translations) before calling Lazarus out of the tomb, still wrapped
in graveclothes. This is the seventh and climactic sign in John's account, unique to this Gospel,
and the narrative hinge that leads directly to the religious authorities' decision to move against
Jesus (John 11:45-53, not selected for challenge content here).

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 11:21 (WEB): "Therefore Martha said to Jesus, 'Lord, if you would have been here, my brother
  wouldn't have died.'"
- John 11:25-26 (WEB): "Jesus said to her, 'I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in
  me will still live, even if he dies. Whoever lives and believes in me will never die. Do you
  believe this?'"
- John 11:35 (WEB): "Jesus wept."
- John 11:43-44 (WEB, trimmed): "He cried with a loud voice, 'Lazarus, come out!' He who was dead
  came out, bound hand and foot with wrappings... Jesus said to them, 'Free him, and let him go.'"

### 11. Washing the Disciples' Feet and the New Commandment (John 13:1-35)
At the last meal before his crucifixion, Jesus rises from the table, takes a towel, and washes his
disciples' feet — a task normally done by the lowest household servant — telling them to do the
same for one another. He gives what he calls "a new commandment": to love one another as he has
loved them, stating this love is how everyone will recognize them as his disciples. Unique to
John's account of the meal (John does not narrate the bread-and-cup institution words found in the
synoptics — see Contested Territory).

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 13:4-5 (WEB, trimmed): "He took a towel and wrapped a towel around his waist. Then he poured
  water into the basin, and began to wash the disciples' feet and to wipe them with the towel."
- John 13:14-15 (WEB): "If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought
  to wash one another's feet. For I have given you an example, that you should also do as I have
  done to you."
- John 13:34 (WEB): "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another. Just as I have
  loved you, you also love one another."

### 12. The Farewell Discourse: "The Way, the Truth, and the Life" and the True Vine (John 14:1-6, 15:1-13)
John alone preserves an extended, multi-chapter farewell address (chs. 14-17) Jesus gives his
disciples the night before his death, promising he goes to prepare a place for them and identifying
himself as "the way, the truth, and the life." He also describes himself as "the true vine," his
disciples as branches, and states that remaining in him is what allows a disciple to "bear much
fruit." This brief draws on the discourse's two best-known passages (14:1-6, 15:1-13) as its
representative sample of this uniquely-John material rather than covering all four chapters.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 14:2-3 (WEB, trimmed): "In my Father's house are many homes... I go to prepare a place for
  you. If I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again and will receive you to myself."
- John 14:6 (WEB): "I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except
  through me."
- John 15:5 (WEB): "I am the vine. You are the branches. He who remains in me and I in him bears
  much fruit, for apart from me you can do nothing."
- John 15:13 (WEB): "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his
  friends."

### 13. The Crucifixion: Distinctively John's Details (John 19:16-34)
John's crucifixion account shares the core event with the synoptics but this brief draws
specifically on the details unique to John's telling: Jesus carrying his own cross to Golgotha, his
final words "It is finished," and the detail — reported nowhere else — of a soldier piercing his
side with a spear after his death, from which "blood and water" came out.

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 19:17-18 (WEB, trimmed): "He went out, bearing his cross, to the place called 'The Place of
  a Skull'... where they crucified him, and with him two others, on either side one, and Jesus in
  the middle."
- John 19:30 (WEB): "When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, 'It is finished!' Then
  he bowed his head and gave up his spirit."
- John 19:34 (WEB): "However, one of the soldiers pierced his side with a spear, and immediately
  blood and water came out."

### 14. The Empty Tomb and Doubting Thomas (John 20:1-29)
Mary Magdalene finds the tomb empty and, weeping outside it, is the first to see and speak with the
risen Jesus, mistaking him for the gardener until he says her name. Thomas, absent when Jesus first
appears to the other disciples, says he will not believe without touching Jesus's wounds; eight
days later Jesus appears again, invites Thomas to touch his hands and side, and Thomas responds,
"My Lord and my God!" — unique to John and the Gospel's climactic confession of Jesus's identity.
The book then states its own purpose (20:30-31, not selected as a challenge item but noted as the
book's own stated thesis: these signs are written "that you may believe").

**Challenge-ready verses:**
- John 20:1-2 (WEB, trimmed): "Mary Magdalene went early, while it was still dark, to the tomb, and
  saw that the stone had been taken away from the tomb. Therefore she ran and came to Simon Peter
  and to the other disciple... and said to them, 'They have taken away the Lord out of the tomb.'"
- John 20:16 (WEB): "Jesus said to her, 'Mary.' She turned and said to him, 'Rabboni!' which is to
  say, 'Teacher!'"
- John 20:25 (WEB, trimmed): "'Unless I see in his hands the print of the nails, put my finger into
  the print of the nails, and put my hand into his side, I will not believe.'"
- John 20:28 (WEB): "Thomas answered him, 'My Lord and my God!'"
- John 20:29 (WEB): "Jesus said to him, 'Because you have seen me, you have believed. Blessed are
  those who have not seen and have believed.'"

Boss battle (world 43 capstone): a mixed-format sequence spanning all 14 pivotal scenes, weighted
toward level 3-5 items. Given this book's unusually high concentration of format-restricted verses

multiple-choice items than in a less doctrinally dense book like Genesis or the historical books —
similar to how Hosea's marriage-narrative bounding pushed its own ladder toward recall, and
consistent with this being the first Gospel authored under the NT's stricter contested-territory

## Contested Territory

Per `docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §4 and §6 (New Testament Addendum) — flagged for the theological
reviewer, not resolved here.

- **The Prologue's high Christology (John 1:1-18), especially 1:1 ("the Word was God") and 1:14
  ("the Word became flesh").** This is the single densest piece of Trinitarian/incarnational
  language in the entire Bible and is core, load-bearing narrative text for John's world — it
  cannot be cut and still be authoring John's Gospel. **Bounding:** every challenge item built from
  this passage tests only the verbatim text itself (recall/fill-in-blank) or the bare narrative
  fact the text states (the Word existed with God, was God, and became flesh) — no challenge item
  asks the player to affirm, explain, or resolve any specific historic Trinitarian formulation (the
  Nicene Creed's language, the nature of the hypostatic union, *homoousios* vs. subordinate
  readings, or any post-biblical theological vocabulary at all). The verse states what it states;
  this brief doesn't ask a player to go further than the verse does. **Structural rule: John 1:1,
  1:3, and 1:14 are restricted to recall/fill-in-blank format only — never a multiple-choice "what
  does this mean" or "who/what is the Word" format.**

- **"I am" self-identification statements with divine-name resonance (John 8:58, "before Abraham
  came into existence, I AM"; John 10:30, "I and the Father are one").** Both verses are widely
  read across mainstream Christian tradition as claims of divine identity (echoing Exodus 3:14's
  "I AM WHO I AM"), but the *theological framework* for explaining exactly how that claim relates to
  Jesus's humanity, and how it should be formally articulated, is itself the same contested
  Christological/Trinitarian ground as the Prologue. **Bounding, same rule as the Prologue:**
  challenge items test only the verbatim text — what Jesus is quoted as saying — never a
  multiple-choice item asking what the statement "means" theologically or requiring the player to
  affirm a specific account of Jesus's nature. **Structural rule: John 8:58 and 10:30 are restricted
  to recall/fill-in-blank format only.**

- **John 20:28, Thomas's "My Lord and my God!"** The Gospel's climactic confession, addressed
  directly to Jesus. Widely read across Christian tradition as Thomas affirming Jesus's divinity;
  the verse's *plain narrative content* (this is what Thomas said, in this scene, after touching
  Jesus's wounds) is uncontested as a report of what the text narrates. **Bounding:** this verse may
  be used in recall format and in narrative-sequence format (placing it correctly in the Thomas
  scene's sequence of events), but not in a multiple-choice format asking what Thomas's words mean
  or imply theologically. **Structural rule: John 20:28 is restricted to recall/fill-in-blank and
  sequence-only format — never a multiple-choice "what did Thomas mean" format.**

- **John 7:53-8:11, the woman caught in adultery — live textual-criticism issue.** Verified directly
  against ebible.org's WEB text (see review file): this passage **is printed** in the WEB text at
  its traditional location, but carries an explicit editorial footnote stating that the Nestle-Aland/
  UBS critical text ("NU") "includes John 7:53–John 8:11, but puts brackets around it to indicate
  that the textual critics had less confidence that this was original" — i.e., WEB prints it but
  flags it as textually uncertain, consistent with near-universal modern scholarly consensus that
  the passage is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts and was likely a later insertion
  into the Gospel text, regardless of the story's own antiquity and wide acceptance as authentic to
  the Jesus tradition even among scholars who doubt it belongs in John specifically. **Bounding:
  this brief does not select any verse from John 7:53-8:11 for challenge content, given the live
  manuscript-tradition question independent of any doctrinal one — the same conservative default
  posture the style guide's NT addendum recommends for known textual-criticism cases.** This is a
  content-availability decision (excluded from challenges), not a claim that the passage is
  inauthentic or unimportant to Christian tradition generally.

- **Sacramental theology — the Bread of Life discourse (John 6:22-59), not selected for challenge
  content beyond 6:35.** This is John's actual locus of the real-presence-vs-memorial Eucharistic
  debate, not (as an earlier draft of this brief assumed) the absence of a Last-Supper institution
  narrative. John does not include the synoptics' "this is my body / this is my blood" words at the
  meal itself (ch. 13 narrates the foot-washing and farewell discourse instead), but chapter 6's
  extended discourse — especially verses 51-58, where Jesus says "unless you eat the flesh of the
  Son of Man and drink his blood, you don't have life in yourselves" — is historically the real
  sacramental fault line: Catholic and Orthodox tradition reads this passage as bearing directly on
  the Eucharist/real presence, while much of Protestant tradition reads it as figurative language
  about faith and spiritual reliance on Christ, not a reference to a future sacrament. **Bounding:
  this brief selects only John 6:35** ("I am the bread of life... whoever comes to me will not be
  hungry") **for challenge content from this discourse — confirmed via source check (see review
  file) that verse 35 itself is read consistently across commentary traditions as spiritual/
  relational language ("coming to Jesus" and "believing" as parallel terms), with the sacramental
  divide specific to the chapter's later, more concrete "eat my flesh, drink my blood" language
  (6:51-58), which this brief does not select.** This is a deliberate scope boundary, not an
  oversight: no challenge item in this brief touches John 6:51-58.

- **Soteriology / faith-vs-works.** John's Gospel emphasizes belief ("that you may believe," John
  20:31; "whoever believes in him," 3:16) far more than any explicit works-language comparable to
  James 2. No verse selected in this brief creates a faith-vs-works tension requiring bounding;
  this category is more directly live in Romans/Galatians/James (later NT waves) than in John. Noted
  here as actively considered per the style guide, not because a specific bounding was needed.

- **Atonement theory.** John narrates the crucifixion's events (19:16-34) without the book itself
  offering an explicit theological account of *how* the crucifixion accomplishes salvation (no
  "ransom," ancient sacrificial-substitution, or similar explanatory language attached to the
  selected verses) — the closest the book comes is John the Baptist's "Lamb of God, who takes away
  the sin of the world" (1:29), which itself doesn't specify a mechanism. **Bounding:** John 1:29
  and the crucifixion verses selected above are used only to report what happened and what was
  said, never to grade a specific theory (penal substitution, Christus Victor, ransom, or any other)
  of how or why it accomplishes what it accomplishes.

- **Ecclesiology / gender roles, spiritual gifts, eschatology.** None of these categories are
  directly live in the material selected for this brief. John's Gospel narrates almost no church-
  order material (that develops later, in Acts and the epistles) and no extended eschatological
  discourse comparable to the synoptics' Olivet Discourse (John's closest analog, the Farewell
  Discourse's promise of the Spirit in 14:16-17, is included above and handled as narrative report
  only — what Jesus says he will do — not as a challenge item requiring a position on the Spirit's
  ongoing work). Noted here as actively considered per the style guide's instruction not to wait to
  be surprised by these categories, not because a bounding was needed in this brief.

- **John 9's extended interrogation scene (9:8-41, not selected for challenge content).** The
  chapter's second half is a lengthy, sometimes sharply worded exchange between the healed man and
  religious authorities that touches historically fraught Jewish-Christian polemic territory
  (particularly later verses referring to "the Jews" as a hostile faction, a recurring stylistic
  feature of John's Gospel that has been misused historically in antisemitic ways it was not
  written to support). This brief selects only the healing itself (9:1-7) and the man's simple,
  uncontested testimony (9:25), and does not draw any challenge content from the interrogation
  scene — a deliberate scope choice given the sensitivity of that material, not an oversight.

- **John 3:5, "born of water and Spirit" (not selected for challenge content).** Confirmed via
  source check (see review file) that this specific phrase is a genuine, live Catholic/Orthodox vs.
  Protestant sacramental fault line — Catholic and Orthodox tradition reads "water" as referring to
  baptism (the Council of Trent formalized this reading), while much of Protestant tradition (Calvin
  is a representative example) reads "water" as figurative for spiritual cleansing, not a reference
  to the sacrament. **This brief selects only John 3:3** ("born anew... can't see God's Kingdom"),
  confirmed to carry no comparable cross-tradition dispute, **and does not select 3:5** — a
  deliberate scope boundary, not an oversight.

- **John's broader "the Jews" language, more generally.** John's Gospel uses "the Jews" (Greek *hoi
  Ioudaioi*) more frequently than the other Gospels, often to refer to a specific hostile faction of
  religious authorities rather than the Jewish people as a whole — a usage widely recognized by
  Christian and Jewish scholars alike as historically weaponized in ways the text's original context
  does not support. This brief avoids the practical risk entirely by construction: no challenge-
  ready verse selected anywhere above uses this phrase, and no challenge item characterizes any
  group in the text. Flagged here explicitly so the reviewer checks it deliberately rather than it
  going unmentioned.
