## 3. Contested-Territory Cross-Check

### 3.1 Mark 16:9-20, the "Longer Ending" — confirmed genuine, live, three-way split; exclusion from challenge content confirmed necessary

This is the specific, named textual-criticism case (`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7,
`docs/CONTENT_STYLE_GUIDE.md` §6) this assignment required be handled directly. Checked from three
independent angles, deliberately spanning traditions:

- **Manuscript/patristic evidence, via classic cross-tradition commentary aggregation:**
  [BibleHub's commentary aggregation on Mark 16:9](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mark/16-9.htm)
  confirms the passage is "not found in two of the oldest MSS.—the Sinaitic and the Vatican," while
  being "found in the Alexandrian and Cambridge MSS., and in twelve other uncials." Patristic
  testimony is mixed: Eusebius and Jerome both reported that "the correct" or "nearly all" Greek
  copies known to them omitted verses 9-20, while Irenaeus (c. AD 170) is cited as referencing the
  passage, indicating some form of it circulated early. Commentators surveyed (Ellicott, Meyer)
  largely conclude the verses are **not original to Mark's own composition**, with Meyer terming them
  "the apocryphal fragment of some other evangelical treatise."
- **Catholic position, direct primary source:** the [USCCB study note on Mark
  16](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/mark/16) states plainly that this "Longer Ending" section's
  "vocabulary and style indicate that it was written by someone other than Mark" — i.e., mainstream
  Catholic biblical scholarship agrees with the non-Catholic critical consensus on authorship —
  **but** the note also states the passage "has traditionally been accepted as a canonical part of
  the gospel and was defined as such by the Council of Trent." This is a genuinely important
  finding: the Catholic Church holds a position that separates *compositional origin* (not Mark's
  own writing) from *canonical status* (authoritative Scripture regardless), which is a distinct
  category from how the passage's status is typically framed in Protestant discussion.
- **Evangelical/Protestant position, direct primary source:**
  [GotQuestions.org's article on Mark 16:9-20](https://www.gotquestions.org/Mark-16-9-20.html)
  states "many scholars, from all theological persuasions, consider Mark 16:9-20 to be a spurious
  addition to Mark's Gospel," citing the same Sinaiticus/Vaticanus evidence, plus internal stylistic
  evidence (18 words appearing nowhere else in Mark, abrupt transitions). Its explicit conclusion:
  "it is unwise to base a doctrine solely on what is found in this section of Mark" — directly
  relevant to the passage's snake-handling/poison-drinking material (16:17-18), which the source
  notes has been used historically to justify practices (e.g. serpent-handling in some Appalachian
  churches) that most of Christianity rejects as normative.
- **WEB's own translator note, confirmed live (§1 above):** WEB's translators, unlike the mainstream
  modern-critical consensus reflected in both the Catholic and Protestant sourcing above, state they
  regard the passage as reliable specifically on **Majority Text/Textus Receptus-priority** grounds
  — a real, minority-but-not-fringe position within textual criticism (associated with the Byzantine
  priority school), not an idiosyncratic choice invented for this project.

**Finding: this is a genuine, three-way-documented textual and canonical-status dispute, not a
strawman, and the brief's decision to exclude all of 16:9-20 from challenge-ready content — while
still directly documenting the dispute in both Scene 13 and Contested Territory rather than silently
omitting it — is the correct, necessary, and sufficient bounding.** Building any challenge item from
16:9-20 would require presenting contested material as settled "the text of Mark" in exactly the way
the style guide's citation requirement (§5) and paraphrase rule (§3) are designed to prevent, given
that even WEB's own inclusion is explicitly a stated editorial position rather than an absence of
dispute. No change needed; the brief's handling already matches what this review independently
confirms.

### 3.2 Mark 10:1-12, divorce — confirmed genuine three-way split, bounding holds

- **Catholic position, direct primary source:** the Catechism of the Catholic Church, §1614-1615
  (confirmed via [scborromeo.org's CCC
  text](https://www.scborromeo.org/ccc/p2s2c3a7.htm) and corroborating search results), states that
  Jesus "unequivocally taught the original meaning of the union of man and woman as the Creator
  willed it," that Moses' divorce permission was "a concession to the hardness of hearts," and that
  the marital union is indissoluble — directly citing "what therefore God has joined together, let
  no man put asunder," the same clause this brief quotes from Mark 10:9.
- **Protestant/evangelical position:** [GotQuestions.org's overview of divorce and
  remarriage](https://www.gotquestions.org/divorce-remarriage.html) confirms mainstream evangelical
  teaching generally permits divorce and remarriage in cases of sexual immorality (per Matthew's
  parallel "exception clause," not present in Mark's own wording) or abandonment by an unbelieving
  spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15) — a real, substantive difference from the Catholic position's strict
  indissolubility-with-annulment-only framework.
- **Orthodox position:** confirmed via multiple sources (including [OCA's official Q&A on divorce
  and remarriage](https://www.oca.org/questions/sacramentmarriage/divorce-and-remarriage)) that
  Orthodox canon law permits a second or, in economia, even a third marriage, through a distinct,
  penitential remarriage rite — again substantively different from both the Catholic and
  Protestant-evangelical positions above.
- **Finding: the three-way split is real and confirmed, and it bears directly on any question that
  would go beyond Mark's own stated words into "what circumstances permit divorce/remarriage."**
  Mark 10:1-12 itself does not state an exception clause (that appears only in Matthew's parallel).
  The brief's bounding — recall-only format, testing the verbatim text of what Jesus said in Mark's
  own account, never a multiple-choice item about permitted circumstances — is necessary and
  correctly scoped. No change needed.

### 3.3 Mark 10:17-31, the rich young man — confirmed genuine divergence, bounding holds

- **Catholic position:** the [Catholic Encyclopedia's "Evangelical Counsels"
  article](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/04435a.htm), confirmed live, explicitly cites this
  passage (via its Matthew parallel, 19:16-22) as the scriptural root of the "counsels of
  perfection" (poverty, chastity, obedience) — historically the foundation of the church's
  monastic/religious-life tradition — while explicitly stating the counsels "are not binding upon
  all, hence not necessary conditions without which heaven... cannot be attained." This is a
  meaningful, positive theological use of the passage distinct from how it typically functions in
  Protestant preaching.
- **Protestant position:** BibleHub's cross-tradition commentary aggregation on [Mark
  10:21](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/mark/10-21.htm) shows consistent agreement among the
  classic commentators surveyed (Barnes, Bengel, Gill, Cambridge Bible, Pulpit Commentary) that
  Jesus's instruction was a **situational test aimed at this one man's specific attachment to
  wealth**, not a universal condition of salvation — explicitly framed by several of these sources as
  avoiding a works-righteousness reading.
- **Finding: real difference in theological use (a basis for a positive counsels-of-perfection
  doctrine in Catholic tradition vs. a diagnostic-test reading in Protestant commentary), though
  interestingly neither tradition surveyed reads this passage as teaching salvation-by-works in the
  sense the style guide is most concerned about — the sharper soteriological fault line
  (faith-alone vs. faith-and-works as competing accounts of justification) is more directly Paul's
  and James's territory (Romans/Galatians vs. James 2, per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's note),
  which this book does not reach.** The brief's bounding (narrative fact plus recall-only on the
  "sell what you have" instruction, no multiple-choice item on what's required for salvation) is
  still necessary and sufficient given the real Catholic/Protestant divergence that does exist here.
  No change needed.

### 3.4 Mark 10:45, "a ransom for many" — additional item, independently confirmed and bounded

This item was drafted directly into the brief (not flagged only after the fact). Checked its
strength independently:

- Multiple sources confirm this exact verse is the primary proof-text for **three different**
  atonement-theory accounts: classic **ransom theory** (payment to Satan, an early patristic
  reading with continuing resonance in some Orthodox-adjacent framing, per
  [Wikipedia's overview, cross-checked against the ransom-theory literature
  generally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ransom_theory_of_atonement)); **Christus Victor** (victory
  over the powers of evil, without the "payment to Satan" mechanism); and **penal substitution**
  (mainstream evangelical/Reformed reading connecting the verse to Isaiah 53, per
  [The Gospel Coalition's essay on substitutionary
  atonement](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/essay/substitutionary-atonement/)).
- **Finding: this is a genuine, sharply-contested single verse, arguably the clearest atonement-
  theory flashpoint in Mark's own text** (Mark, unlike Romans or Hebrews, does not otherwise theorize
  the mechanism of the cross at length, making this verse do unusually heavy doctrinal lifting for
  its length). The brief's bounding (recall-only, verbatim text of Jesus's stated purpose, no
  multiple-choice item about the mechanism or recipient of the ransom) is necessary and correctly
  scoped. No change needed.

### 3.5 Mark 14:22-24, the Last Supper — confirmed genuine multi-way split, bounding holds

- Confirmed via search-aggregated sourcing spanning multiple traditions (search results drawing on
  [reformedanswers.org/thirdmill.org's comparative
  overview](https://reformedanswers.org/answer.asp/file/46649) and corroborating general search on
  Eastern Orthodox eucharistic theology) that at minimum **five** distinct positions exist on what
  the bread and wine become/do: Catholic transubstantiation; Orthodox real/substantial presence
  (affirmed as fact while explicitly declining, per St. Philaret's catechism, to define the
  mechanism); Lutheran "sacramental union"/real presence "in, with, and under" the elements; Reformed
  spiritual presence (Calvin); and Zwinglian/memorialist views common in much of broader
  evangelicalism, holding no special presence at all.
- **Finding: this is exactly the kind of sharply-diverged sacramental-theology territory the style
  guide addendum names directly.** The brief's bounding (verbatim institution-narrative text only,
  no multiple-choice item on what the elements become or accomplish) is necessary and sufficient. No
  change needed.

### 3.6 Mark 4:11-12, the stated purpose of parables — additional item, confirmed and bounded

The brief independently flagged this as touching predestination/free-will territory, on the same
grounds this project already used to exclude Malachi 1:2-3 (`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's Romans 9
note). Checked this reasoning directly rather than accepting it uncritically: the verse's own
language ("that seeing they may see and not perceive... lest... their sins should be forgiven them")
does carry real weight in Calvinist/Arminian discussions of election and human responsibility if a
challenge item were to ask what the verse means or why some people don't perceive. **Finding: the
brief's caution is well-placed and its bounding (recall-only, no "what does this mean" format) is
appropriate,** though this reviewer notes the connection is somewhat less sharply a live
denominational battle line than 10:45's atonement-theory split or 14:22-24's sacramental split — it
is closer in kind to a background theological undertone than a headline doctrine most lay readers
would immediately recognize as contested. The recall-only restriction is a reasonable, low-cost
precaution regardless, consistent with this project's general practice of erring toward more
restriction rather than less on any verse carrying real interpretive weight. No change needed.

### 3.7 Mark 13, the Olivet Discourse — confirmed correctly excluded

Checked whether excluding all of Mark 13 from challenge content (rather than attempting a narrow
narrative-only bounding, as was done for other contested passages in this brief) was the right call
given the style guide addendum's specific warning about eschatology being "the single most fragmented
interpretive territory in all of Christian theology." Given that even narrating "what happens" in
this discourse requires selecting among readings of highly symbolic language (the "abomination of
desolation," what "this generation" refers to, whether the discourse describes AD 70, a future
end-times scenario, or both), unlike more straightforwardly narratable material elsewhere in this
brief, **this reviewer agrees full exclusion (rather than narrow bounding) was the more defensible
choice** — consistent with how this project's OT briefs have handled comparably dense material by
scope-cutting rather than attempting an interpretation-proof narrative retelling. No change needed.

### 3.8 Ecclesiology/gender-role passages and spiritual gifts — confirmed not applicable / already resolved

Checked the brief's claim that Mark's narrative contains no church-office or gender-role
instructional material: confirmed correct by direct reading of the brief and general knowledge of
Mark's content — that category of material (1 Timothy 2-3, Titus 1, 1 Corinthians 11/14) belongs to
epistles not yet reached by this project. The spiritual-gifts category (tongues, healing,
cessationism/continuationism) is present in Mark only within the excluded 16:9-20 material (§3.1
above resolves this). No additional finding beyond what's already stated in the brief. No change
needed.

## 4. Verdict

**`approved-with-changes`, with the single "change" being a note rather than a content correction —
see below.**

The brief passes on every check performed above: verse-text accuracy (zero mismatches across all 13
scenes, both sources matching for every block); style-guide compliance including correct handling of
Mark's direct divine-sonship material per this project's own NT-specific reasoning
(`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7); and cross-tradition bounding of every contested item this review
checked, including two the brief itself surfaced with strong, independently-confirmed reasoning
(16:9-20's textual dispute, 10:45's atonement-theory flashpoint) and several more this review
independently verified rather than accepted at face value (10:1-12's divorce split, 10:17-31's
counsels-of-perfection divergence, 14:22-24's sacramental split, 4:11-12's lighter-weight
predestination undertone, Mark 13's full exclusion).

**The "changes" component:** this review found no verse requiring reclassification, no format
restriction that was insufficient, and no contested item the brief missed. The one substantive
addition this review makes is **strengthening, not loosening**, one existing note: §3.1 confirms
that the Catholic Church's position on 16:9-20 (non-Markan authorship, per USCCB, but full canonical
status per Trent) is a *third* distinct position beyond the Protestant "likely spurious, don't build
doctrine on it" and WEB's own "reliable per Majority Text" stance — the brief's existing text already
gestures at this three-way complexity but this review's sourcing makes it fully explicit and citable.
No structural change to the brief itself was required as a result — the brief's exclusion of
16:9-20 already correctly anticipated and covered this finding.

**No escalation.** Every contested item in this brief — including the two hardest items, the 16:9-20
textual/canonical dispute and the 10:45 atonement-theory flashpoint — resolves to a bounded,
narrative-only or recall-only structural fix, or (for Mark 13) a scope exclusion, that this reviewer
is confident actually neutralizes the risk, following the same pattern this project has used
successfully across its OT briefs (Esther, 2 Samuel, Ezekiel, Genesis, Hosea) and extended here to
the NT's different contested-category shape per `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's own prediction. No
item was found where genuine cross-tradition disagreement bears on a graded item in a way the
brief's bounding doesn't already handle. No child-safety or content-integrity guardrail concern was
found anywhere in this brief.

**A note on stakes, stated plainly per this reviewer's own standing instruction to be conservative:**
this is one of the first four New Testament books reviewed under this pipeline, running in parallel
with Matthew, Luke, and John. This review was more exhaustive than a typical single-book review would
require in isolation — checking not just whether each bounding holds, but whether it was the *right
kind* of bounding (exclusion vs. narrow format restriction) given how fragmented the underlying
dispute is — specifically because getting this pattern right on the first NT books matters more than
usual for how the remaining 23 NT books' reviews will calibrate. Nothing found here required
escalation, but the reviewer wants Kachi's approval of this book's overall pattern (especially the
16:9-20 exclusion decision and the Mark 13 full-exclusion decision) to be a deliberate choice, not a
rubber stamp, given how much of the rest of this wave will likely follow its precedent.

**Known limitation, stated per the agent definition's own instruction:** this review was performed
by an AI agent standing in for the named human theological reviewer role (Kachi Okere, per Open
Decision #7's resolution). Nothing here is asserted as certain — Kachi may reasonably weigh the
16:9-20 exclusion, the Mark 13 exclusion, or any of the individual format restrictions differently
than this review does.
