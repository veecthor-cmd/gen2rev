## 3. Contested-Territory Cross-Check

### 3.1 The Virgin Birth (Luke 1:26-38) — bounding confirmed correct; confirmed uncontested at the conception-event level across all three primary NT traditions

Checked against multiple sources surveying Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox positions:
per general cross-tradition survey (search results citing the Apostles' Creed, Nicene-
Constantinopolitan Creed, and the Definition of Chalcedon), **the virgin conception itself is
"virtually uncontested" across Protestant, Catholic, and Orthodox tradition** — all three affirm
conception by the Holy Spirit without Joseph's biological involvement. Where traditions genuinely
diverge is downstream of the conception event this brief narrates: Mary's *perpetual* virginity
after Jesus's birth (Catholic and Orthodox tradition affirm it; most Protestant traditions do not
take a firm position or read Jesus's "brothers" as later-born children of Mary and Joseph) and
Mary's own sinlessness (Catholic Immaculate Conception doctrine; not shared by Orthodox or
Protestant tradition in the same form). **The brief does not touch either of these downstream
questions** — it stops at 1:38, Mary's consent, and does not narrate anything about her life or
status after this point. **Finding: the brief's scope (the conception event only) sidesteps the
actual points of cross-tradition divergence entirely**, and the recall-only restriction on 1:32-33
and 1:34-35 correctly prevents any multiple-choice item from drifting into "what does this mean"
territory where a genuinely contested claim (about the nature of the sonship, e.g.) could surface.
**No change needed.**

### 3.2 The Baptism and the Voice from Heaven (Luke 3:21-22) — bounding confirmed necessary

"You are my beloved Son" is a divine-sonship declaration read across all three traditions as
Trinity-adjacent (Father's voice, Son present bodily, Spirit descending "in a bodily form like a
dove," 3:22) — the same structural shape the Genesis review flagged for Genesis 1:27 as prudent to
restrict even absent an active dispute. **Finding: recall-only restriction is a reasonable prudence
measure, consistent with precedent. No change needed.**

### 3.3 The Nazareth Sermon's Self-Application (Luke 4:16-30, esp. 4:21) — bounding confirmed necessary and correctly scoped

Per search-aggregated commentary, Isaiah 61's "Spirit of the Lord" language was recognized by
first-century Jewish hearers as messianic in a general sense — "the very definition of 'messiah'" —
but Jesus's specific self-application of it to himself, and his "today, this Scripture has been
fulfilled" claim, is precisely **the primary NT text's own Christological claim**, per
`docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7's framing: unlike Hosea 11:1 (excluded from NT-fulfillment framing
because *Matthew*, an external later reading, makes that connection, not Hosea's own text), here
Luke's own narrative has Jesus himself making the claim in the story. This is exactly the kind of
claim §7 says is now in-bounds to narrate but not to grade as resolved fact. **Finding: the brief's
bounding (recall-only on 4:21, narrating that Jesus made this claim and quoting it, never grading
whether it's true) is the correct application of §7's own stated principle. No change needed.**

### 3.4 Atonement Theory and the Last Supper's Institution Words (Luke 22:19-20) — bounding confirmed necessary; genuine, deep, still-practiced divide confirmed

This is the single deepest cross-tradition divide this review checked, and the one most different in
kind from anything the OT briefs faced — not a historical interpretive question but a live doctrine
still shaping how these traditions practice the Eucharist/Lord's Supper today:

- **Catholic position** (per [USCCB's own study note on Luke 22](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/22),
  fetched directly): the note frames Luke's account as closely paralleling 1 Corinthians 11:23-26's
  institution tradition and flags the manuscript question at 22:19b-20 (see §3.9 below) without
  asserting a particular resolution to the presence question in the note itself, though Catholic
  doctrine elsewhere holds to transubstantiation.
- **Orthodox position** (per aggregated search results from Orthodox parish and diocesan sources):
  affirms "real, substantial presence of Christ in the Eucharist," produced through the Holy
  Spirit's action in the whole liturgy rather than a single formula, while explicitly holding that
  "this mystery transcends precise doctrinal formulation."
- **Protestant positions, genuinely split internally** (per BibleHub's commentary aggregation on
  [Luke 22:19](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/luke/22-19.htm)): Reformed commentators (Meyer)
  argue for a symbolic reading; Lutheran-tradition commentary (Bengel) leans toward a real-but-
  spiritual presence; the Pulpit Commentary explicitly criticizes *both* Catholic transubstantiation
  *and* Lutheran consubstantiation as over-specifying "Divine mystery." This confirms real presence
  vs. memorial is not even a clean Catholic/Orthodox-vs-Protestant split — it runs *through*
  Protestant tradition itself (Lutheran vs. Reformed vs. Zwinglian/Baptist memorialism).
- **Finding: this is a genuine, live, doctrinally central, still-practiced divide.** The brief's
  bounding — recall-only format, testing only that Jesus said these specific words, never what they
  mean or accomplish — is the correct and, per style guide §2's own "direct quotations attributed
  in-text" category, explicitly sanctioned way to handle this: quoting exact words verbatim is
  categorically different from grading their theological meaning. **This reviewer considered
  escalating this specific item** given how much more practice-relevant it is than any prior
  contested item this project has bounded (Hosea 6:2's "third day," by contrast, has no live liturgical
  practice riding on it) — but concluded the recall-only restriction genuinely neutralizes the risk,
  because the style guide already draws exactly this line (verbatim quotation is in-bounds;
  interpretation is not) and the brief's structural rule enforces it without exception. **No change
  needed, but flagged as the item this review weighed most carefully before declining to escalate.**

### 3.5 "Today You Will Be with Me in Paradise" (Luke 23:42-43) — bounding confirmed necessary; two distinct live divides confirmed

- **Timing/nature of "Paradise":** per aggregated search results including a Catholic Answers/
  Catholic Stand source and Orthodox theological framing, **Eastern Orthodox tradition holds
  "Paradise" is a resting place "on the way" to final glorification, not the final state itself**,
  while mainstream Catholic and Protestant theology generally treats it as the "intermediate state"
  prior to bodily resurrection — a real, if narrower, difference in how the same word is understood
  eschatologically. A minority of Protestant scholars (per the search results, citing N.T. Wright-
  adjacent and other sources) read "Paradise" here as Sheol/Hades rather than the immediate presence
  of God, a genuinely fringe-but-published reading.
- **Faith-vs-works/soteriological reading:** the thief receives the promise with no baptism, no
  church membership, and no completed "work" — a passage Protestant tradition (especially in
  sola fide-focused preaching) has long emphasized as a strong faith-alone case, while Catholic and
  Orthodox tradition harmonize it without treating it as overturning sacramental or synergistic
  frameworks elsewhere in their theology.
- **Finding: both divides are genuine and confirmed.** The brief's recall-only restriction (testing
  only that Jesus said this exact line, never what "Paradise" refers to, when it is entered, or what
  the episode proves about how salvation works) correctly neutralizes both. **No change needed.**

### 3.6 The Resurrection Announcement and the Empty Tomb (Luke 24:1-12) — bounding confirmed correct; confirmed uncontested as narrative across traditions, contested only at the historical-critical layer already handled by policy

Checked [BibleHub's commentary aggregation on Luke 24:5-6](https://biblehub.com/commentaries/luke/24-6.htm)
directly: commentaries across the traditions represented there show **no genuine disagreement about
the narrative content** of the resurrection announcement — all treat "Jesus rose, angels announced
it, this fulfilled prior predictions" as the shared textual claim; the only variation found was a
complementary detail about which prior prediction is referenced (a textual-harmony note, not a
doctrinal dispute). The live question here is not cross-tradition disagreement in the OT sense at
all — it's the historical-critical question of whether the resurrection *happened*, which is exactly
the category `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7 already resolved as project policy (narrate the primary
text's own claim, without requiring the player to affirm it as resolved historical-critical fact).
**Finding: the brief correctly applies existing project policy here. The recall-only restriction on
24:5-6 specifically is not strictly required by a cross-tradition-disagreement analysis (there isn't
one, per the source above) — but this review keeps it as applied, since it also serves the separate,
still-valid §7 purpose of not letting a multiple-choice format imply the claim is being offered for
the player to affirm or deny as fact.** No change needed; the brief's own stated rationale (matching
the virgin-birth treatment) is sound.

### 3.7 Ecclesiology and Gender: Women as First Witnesses (Luke 24:1-11) — checked, correctly left in open format

Checked aggregated search results including [The Gospel Coalition's coverage of female
eyewitnesses](https://www.thegospelcoalition.org/article/female-eyewitnesses-resurrection/) and a
BYU New Testament Commentary treatment: sources broadly agree the narrative fact itself (women found
the tomb first, reported it, were initially disbelieved per 24:11's "nonsense") is **not contested
as narrative** across traditions — it is, if anything, cited across traditions as a point of
apologetic significance (the argument that a fabricated first-century story would not have made
low-credibility-status women its key witnesses). The genuinely contested question — whether this
scene has implications for women's teaching or ecclesial authority today — is a live debate, but one
this brief's items don't engage; they test only who found the tomb and what happened. **Finding: the
brief's choice to leave this in open format (not recall-restricted) is correct — the narrative fact
itself carries no live disagreement, only an inference the brief doesn't draw. No change needed.**

### 3.8 Zacchaeus (19:1-10) and the Prodigal Son (15:11-32) — soteriology check, not separately flagged with full weight in the draft brief; confirmed adequately bounded, no structural change needed

This review independently checked these two scenes against the faith-vs-works soteriology category
the style guide's addendum names, since the draft brief's Contested Territory section did not
originally give either its own dedicated entry (both were covered only implicitly under the general
narrative-fact framing):

- **Zacchaeus:** confirmed a genuine Catholic/Protestant difference in emphasis — Catholic sources
  frame Zacchaeus's restitution as morally necessary "simple justice" flowing from grace-initiated
  conversion; Protestant sources frame the restitution as *evidence* of conversion rather than a
  precondition for it, with some Protestant commentators noting "the Bible can be descriptive
  without being prescriptive" on this point.
- **Prodigal Son:** confirmed a genuine Catholic/Calvinist difference — Catholic reading treats the
  son's decision to return and confess as modeling the sacrament of confession/penance; Calvinist
  reading emphasizes the father's initiating, unconditional response as illustrating irresistible
  grace, with the son's own agency read as secondary.
- **Finding: both are genuine, live differences, but neither bears on a graded item in this brief.**
  Every challenge-ready verse selected from these two scenes (19:5, 19:8, 19:9-10, 15:4, 15:7,
  15:18-19, 15:20, 15:22-24) tests narrative sequence or verbatim recall of what was said and done —
  none asks whether the restitution or the return *caused*, *earned*, or was a *precondition for*
  either character's acceptance. **This finding did not require a structural change to the brief**
  (unlike Hosea 6:2, where the draft's existing hedge was insufficient and needed a hard restriction)
  — the existing narrative-fact-only item selection already avoids the fault line. Noted here so the
  finding is on record, consistent with this role's standing instruction to surface items
  independently rather than rely on the brief's own self-assessment.

### 3.9 Textual-Criticism Findings (Luke 22:19b-20, 22:43-44, 23:34a) — independently researched and confirmed

The draft brief flagged 22:43-44 and 23:34a from this reviewer's own general knowledge without yet
grounding either in a dedicated live source, and did not flag 22:19b-20 at all. This review
independently researched all three:

- **Luke 22:19b-20 (Western non-interpolation):** confirmed directly from the [Catholic USCCB's own
  study note on Luke 22](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/luke/22) — some Western manuscripts omit
  "which is given for you... do this in memory of me" and the cup saying, while "older and more
  widely distributed manuscripts," including "the oldest papyrus manuscript of Luke," carry the
  longer reading WEB prints. **This was a genuine gap in the draft brief** — added to the brief's
  textual-criticism entry during this review (see `docs/content/luke.md`, now covering three
  passages instead of two).
- **Luke 22:43-44:** confirmed via multiple independent textual-criticism sources, including an
  [academic journal article specifically on this passage](https://www.academia.edu/10284742/_Luke_22_43_44_An_Anti_Docetic_Interpolation_or_an_Apologetic_Omission_TC_A_Journal_of_Biblical_Textual_Criticism_19_2014_1_35)
  and the [Evangelical Textual Criticism blog's treatment](http://evangelicaltextualcriticism.blogspot.com/2018/03/the-drops-of-blood-and-angel-in-luke.html):
  absent from key early manuscripts (𝔓69, 𝔓75, and Codex Vaticanus among them), present in Codex
  Sinaiticus and others; scholarly debate continues over whether the passage was original and later
  excised (for apologetic reasons, since it shows Jesus in visible distress) or added later. Not
  selected as a challenge-ready verse in this brief, so no format restriction is required — the
  brief's disclosure is for transparency only.
- **Luke 23:34a ("Father, forgive them"):** confirmed via multiple independent sources, including
  [Alpha and Omega Ministries' dedicated treatment](https://www.aomin.org/aoblog/textual-issues/from-the-lips-of-jesus-or-a-scribal-hand-father-forgive-them-for-they-do-not-know-what-they-are-doing/)
  and the [Bart Ehrman Blog's treatment](https://ehrmanblog.org/did-jesus-pray-father-forgive-them-from-the-cross/):
  absent from "the two strongest Alexandrian witnesses," P75 and Codex Vaticanus, among others, but
  present in "a massive majority of Greek manuscripts representing multiple transmission-branches"
  and "very ancient and very widespread patristic evidence." Genuinely and actively debated among
  textual critics today, not a settled question either direction.
- **Finding: all three findings are confirmed genuine per independent, dedicated sourcing — this is
  the one substantive addition this review made to the brief (adding 22:19b-20 to the existing
  22:43-44/23:34a entry, with citations for all three).** None requires a new challenge-item format
  restriction beyond what the brief's doctrinal bounding (§3.4, and 23:34's inclusion under the
  crucifixion scene's general narrative-fact framing) already provides — this is a text-integrity
  disclosure matter, not a doctrinal-neutrality one, consistent with how the style guide instructs
  handling Mark 16:9-20 and John 7:53-8:11 (verify what WEB prints, disclose the issue, don't
  exclude by default).

### 3.10 Sacraments/Ordinances Beyond the Last Supper: Baptism (Luke 3:1-22) — checked, bounding confirmed sufficient

John's baptism is textually distinguished within Luke's own narrative from later Christian baptism
(3:16's "he will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire" explicitly marks John's water baptism as
preparatory to something else still future in the narrative). The mode/timing/regeneration-vs-symbol
questions the style guide names center on *Christian* baptism, which Acts (not Luke) narrates the
church actually practicing. **Finding: the brief's items (3:7-8, 3:16, 3:21-22) test only narrated
content about John's preaching and Jesus's own baptism, never a claim about what baptism
theologically accomplishes. No change needed; deferred appropriately to the Acts brief.**

### 3.11 Spiritual Gifts, Predestination/Free Will, Eschatology — checked, "not primarily present" / "deliberately excluded" framing confirmed appropriate

- **Spiritual gifts:** confirmed Luke's own text has no parallel to 1 Corinthians 12-14's content on
  gifts distributed among believers; the brief's "deferred to Acts and 1 Corinthians" framing is
  accurate to the book's actual content.
- **Predestination/free will:** confirmed Luke's Gethsemane scene (22:41-42) is narrowly
  Christological (Jesus's own will in his own passion) rather than a general doctrine of human
  predestination in the way Romans 9 raises it; the brief's narrow framing is accurate.
- **Eschatology (Luke 21, deliberately excluded):** confirmed Luke 21 is structurally the same kind
  of Olivet-discourse material as Matthew 24/Mark 13, carrying the same
  pre-/mid-/post-tribulation and millennium-view freight `docs/CANON_STRUCTURE.md` §7 names as "the
  single most fragmented interpretive territory in all of Christian theology." **This review agrees
  with the brief's pre-emptive, whole-chapter exclusion** rather than attempting a narrow bounding —
  the same conservative posture §7 reserves for Revelation-adjacent material, applied here
  appropriately given the parallel-Gospel risk (Matthew and Mark's separately-authored briefs will
  need to make the identical call on their own parallel passages).

## 4. Verdict

**`approved-with-changes`.** The brief passes on every check performed above — verse-text accuracy
(zero mismatches across all eighteen scenes, with every doctrinally load-bearing block confirmed
against two independent sources), style-guide compliance, and cross-tradition bounding of every
contested item this review checked, including three items it independently surfaced or strengthened
beyond what the draft brief itself flagged with full weight (§3.8's Zacchaeus/Prodigal Son
soteriology check, §3.9's addition of the Luke 22:19b-20 textual-criticism finding with full
citations for all three manuscript-disputed passages).

**The "changes" component is narrow and has already been applied to the brief, not left as a
follow-up:** per §3.9 above, `docs/content/luke.md`'s textual-criticism Contested Territory entry
now covers three manuscript-disputed passages (22:19b-20, 22:43-44, 23:34a) instead of two, each
with independent citations, matching the same pattern the Genesis and Hosea reviews used of applying
recommended fixes directly rather than leaving them open.

**No escalation — but one item was seriously weighed and is recorded here for transparency:** per
§3.4, the Last Supper's institution words (22:19-20) sit on the single deepest and most
practice-relevant doctrinal fault line this project has encountered so far — the real-presence
question that, historically, split the Reformation itself and still shapes how Catholic, Orthodox,
and different Protestant traditions practice communion today. This reviewer considered escalating
this specific item rather than resolving it via format restriction alone, given how much higher the
stakes are here than any prior recall-only precedent (Hosea 6:2, Genesis 3:15) — none of which
